# Podcast digest — 2026-07-10

20 new episodes across 14 podcasts since the last digest.

The thread running through this window is leverage — who actually has it, and what it costs to use it against someone bigger. A 24-year-old who founded her AI-safety nonprofit at 15 describes OpenAI subpoenaing her general counsel at his own dinner table, and wins anyway. Kalshi's founders sue their federal regulator against the advice of nearly everyone around them, and win three and a half weeks before an election. A private Discord of amateur election forecasters out-calls the professionals who get paid for it. Underneath all three is the same AI buildout compounding in the background — one hedge fund's internal token usage is up 86-fold since January, Chinese labs are quietly out-executing the "AI psychosis" rhetoric coming out of Silicon Valley, and Booking.com is spending nine figures automating travel while insisting some humans stay on the phones. And running alongside the power stuff, a quieter counter-thread about attention: what's lost when you outsource thinking to an assistant, when a $20 sponsorship on a football shirt becomes a $700 billion advertising industry, or when a generation documents a trip instead of taking one.

**Thesis:** Sneha Revanur founded Encode at 15, and six years later her small advocacy nonprofit has helped pass California's SB-53 and New York's RAISE Act by building an "unlikely coalition" of family-first conservatives, grieving mothers, Hollywood actors, and AI safety researchers against a $125-million-funded anti-regulation lobbying apparatus. The relationship with OpenAI turned openly adversarial in 2025 when the company subpoenaed Encode's general counsel, Nathan Calvin, at his own home — a moment that went viral and, paradoxically, legitimized Encode as a serious player in AI politics.

- **The subpoena, served at dinner.** During the SB-53 fight, a sheriff's deputy served Nathan Calvin the subpoena "while he was having dinner with his wife." Encode was subpoenaed both individually and at its corporate address, which Revanur notes is "very much nonstandard." Calvin's thread describing it got 6 million views on Twitter and prompted OpenAI chief strategy officer Jason Kwon to publish his own thread in response.
- **"We're kind of playing in the big leagues now."** Revanur frames the fallout as double-edged: intensely stressful, but also validation that Encode's work was "being taken seriously by people in high places" for the first time.
- **The coalition is the strategy.** Encode's approach depends on stitching together groups that would never otherwise be in the same room — Revanur credits "a lot of mutual trust building" and monthly coordination calls run by a colleague, Adam, engaging "hundreds of allies" from orgs like Americans for Responsible Innovation.
- **Not an "AGI-pilled" opponent.** Revanur argues the industry's anti-regulation lobbying — funded partly through groups like Fairshake, whose architects include OpenAI's Chris Lehane and a16z — is fundamentally miscalibrated: "This is fundamentally not an AGI-pilled strategy... the stuff that LTF is doing is not what you would do if you saw the writing on the wall that AI is going to be a big deal politically."
- **Wins can look like losses.** Even vetoed bills, Revanur argues, can still provide strategic wins by shifting the Overton window and building relationships for the next legislative cycle — this being Encode's "third preemption rodeo."

> "This is fundamentally not an AGI-pilled strategy. Honestly, it's funny how often you can completely demystify why some person is acting a certain way if you just model them as not being sufficiently AGI-pilled." — Sneha Revanur

### Odd Lots — "These Are the Sharps Actually Making Money on Prediction Markets"

**Guests:** Brian Golden and Daniel Reichman (traders, "Maga Kiwi Club" Discord), Adam Iscoe (journalist, *The New York Times Magazine*) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-07-06 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/these-are-the-sharps-actually-making-money-on-prediction-markets)

**Thesis:** A group of traders in a private Discord called Maga Kiwi Club — most of whom found each other through political forecasting dating back to the 2016–2017 Predictit era — describe how they consistently beat prediction markets on elections through original polling, historical precinct-level modeling, and a deliberate refusal to let media echo chambers shape their beliefs. The clearest proof case: on election night for the Los Angeles mayoral race, the group's internal model correctly bet against Spencer Pratt making the top two, even as right-wing and some mainstream media treated his chances as live.

- **The name is an inside joke, not an ideology.** Golden explains the "Maga Kiwi club" name is ironic; member describes the group's dynamic as "almost an Avengers model" where each trader has a specialty and their own portfolio but shares information collaboratively.

- **Elections are the most mispriced markets, precisely because they're emotional.** Unlike inflation, politics "brings out matters of the heart" and is more subject to media-silo effects — the traders cite the LA mayoral race, where Spencer Pratt's odds of winning spiked on partisan media buzz despite the math ("Los Angeles is a Democrat plus forty-two city") never supporting it.
- **The edge is precinct-level work, not vibes.** On election night for that same race, the group had built precinct-and-region historical models of expected late-vote patterns; while candidate Nithya Raman was reportedly in tears believing she'd lost, the group was betting she'd make the top two — and she did.
- **Insider information is a live, unresolved debate within the group itself**, discussed candidly but without full resolution in the conversation (per show notes framing; not fully captured in the excerpted transcript).

> "I think if they were all efficiently priced, you wouldn't be able to get some of the returns that some people in this group have gotten... > "There wasn't a sharp that I knew that didn't have one of the biggest positions of their lives on Spencer Pratt not winning the mayorship of Los Angeles because the math was just not there to be mathing." — trader, Maga Kiwi Club

### Odd Lots — "One of the World's Largest Hedge Funds on Its 86x Growth in Token Spending"

**Guests:** Gary Collier (CTO) and Tushara Fernando (Head of Data and AI), Man Group · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-07-09 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/one-of-the-worlds-largest-hedge-funds-on-its-86x-growth-in-token-spending)

**Thesis:** Man Group's CTO and head of data/AI describe how one of the world's largest publicly traded hedge funds has seen internal AI token consumption rise 86-fold since January, driven not by top-down mandates but by federated budgets, heavy internal education, and adoption spreading well beyond engineering into finance, operations, and HR. Rather than building an automatic cost-routing classifier to steer staff toward cheaper models, Man Group deliberately chose to teach roughly 1,700–1,800 employees the underlying economics themselves.

- **86x since January, and still accelerating.** "Since January, I think token consumption has gone up eighty six times," Fernando says, describing usage growth as "across the board" — not confined to technical teams.
- **Deliberately no auto-routing classifier.** Despite interest in the industry around building classifiers that route queries to the cheapest sufficient model, Man Group "could do that" but "chosen not to" — instead investing in staff education so people understand which model fits which task themselves.
- **The mistakes were mundane.** Early usage data showed people mixing unrelated tasks in a single context window — drafting an investment thesis, then asking where to go to lunch, then asking about tomorrow's weather, all in the same session — driving up cost for no benefit.
- **Employees found their own efficiency hacks.** One example: intercepting simple git commands so a coding agent doesn't burn tokens processing raw command output as if it were a prose response requiring inference.
- **The METR benchmark as their yardstick.** Fernando cites a benchmark tracking how long a task an agentic workflow can autonomously complete — doubling roughly every seven months — meaning agents can now be handed tasks that would take a human sixteen hours, changing how the firm thinks about team structure.
- **Budgets are fungible, not fixed by seniority or title.** Departments can reallocate other spend into token budgets freely; Man Group's philosophy is pushing decision-making to "the lowest possible level that it makes sense."

> "So since January, I think token consumption has gone up eighty six times... it's been across the board where it's not just been in the tech and tech adjacent departments." — Tushara Fernando, Man Group

### Odd Lots — "The Korean Levered ETFs Shaking Markets All Around the World"

**Guest:** Alexander Altmann (Global Head of Equities Tactical Strategies, Barclays) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-07-10 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/the-korean-levered-etfs-shaking-markets-all-around-the-world)

**Thesis:** Barclays' Alexander Altmann explains why he's called the notional exposure building up in leveraged single-stock ETFs "terrifying" — global AUM in the space hit roughly $250–270 billion at its recent local high, with Asia-Pacific leveraged-product AUM roughly quadrupling this year (from ~$12–13 billion to ~$50–55 billion) largely through new retail money in Korea, versus the U.S., where growth has come mostly from price appreciation rather than new inflows.

- **The mechanics create mechanical, forced flows.** Altmann walks through the math of a triple-levered product: a 10% drop in the underlying forces the fund to mechanically de-lever by a disproportionate amount to maintain its stated leverage ratio, creating a "short gamma dimension" that used to be small and is no longer.
- **Korea is "ground zero" for retail leverage.** 93% of levered ETFs in Korea are owned by retail investors, versus roughly 75% in the U.S. — still high, but comparatively lower.
- **A structural fact, not just a Korea story.** Thirty-four percent of U.S. household wealth is now in equities, the highest on record and 8 percentage points above the next-largest asset class (real estate) — also a record gap.
- **What actually keeps him up at night isn't leveraged ETFs.** Altmann says a 20% equity market impairment would destroy roughly $16 trillion of wealth — about half of U.S. GDP — triggering an immediate consumption shock: "I just think the stock market is the economy now."
- **Balance-sheet tightness has multiple culprits.** He pushes back on the idea that levered ETFs alone are squeezing bank financing rates, pointing instead to rising spot prices generally and multi-manager hedge fund platforms (whose AUM has roughly tripled since COVID) as bigger contributors.

> "We as a society have never been this over-indexed or overexposed to equities." — Alexander Altmann, Barclays

> "I just think the stock market is the economy now — not in the traditional sense... but a twenty percent impairment to the stock market is kind of, I think, [it] will trigger a meaningful downturn in US consumption." — Alexander Altmann, Barclays

**Thesis:** Joey D'Urso traces how the 1994 U.S. World Cup — despite most Americans having little interest in soccer at the time — became the tournament that taught FIFA to commercialize the sport ruthlessly, with sponsors like McDonald's (its first-ever global sponsorship), General Motors, and MasterCard paying up to $20 million to get involved, a sum Harvard Business School later wrote case studies about.

- **FIFA chased money, not fandom.** D'Urso says FIFA "had long wanted to bring the World Cup to the US, even though no one really cared in the US about the sport," because the U.S. held the world's biggest companies, richest people, and largest pool of wealthy consumers.
- **Before 1994, sponsorship was almost accidental.** D'Urso cites a story from the 1974 World Cup in Mexico where a London tailor simply bought up stadium advertising boards nobody else had thought to buy, for a nominal fee — "there's no process, there's no marketing department, there's no anything" in football sponsorship's early days.
- **Alan Rothenberg brought NBA-style commercialization.** The organizer running the U.S. bid, described as coming from an NBA background, turned 1994 into a genuinely commercial event — sponsors included Coca-Cola and Visa at a scale World Cups hadn't previously seen.
- **A World Cup as the world's biggest billboard.** D'Urso frames the real sponsor logic bluntly: for a company like Coca-Cola trying to reach Africa and Asia, the World Cup is "the best billboard you could possibly imagine," reaching villages without electricity.
- **What actually makes a World Cup memorable has little to do with commerce** — D'Urso points to home-team success and personal nostalgia (England's "Gazza's tears" in 1990, Maradona in Argentina) as the real drivers of a tournament's legacy, contrasting the emotional core of the sport with its commercial machinery.
- **A darker aside, deliberately not smoothed over:** D'Urso notes 1978's World Cup was won by Argentina under a military dictatorship "as people were being tortured like a mile down the road" — a fact he says is "slightly airbrushed out of the story."

> "FIFA had long wanted to bring the World Cup to the US, even though no one really cared in the US about the sport. But because of money, because of business, because the world's biggest companies, the world's richest people, the world's biggest pool of wealthy consumers are in the US..." — Joey D'Urso

> "If you're Coca-Cola and you want to sell it in Africa and Asia, [the World Cup] is the best billboard you could possibly imagine." — Joey D'Urso
