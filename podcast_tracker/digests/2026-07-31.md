# Podcast digest — 2026-07-31

17 new episodes across 12 podcasts since the last digest.

Every episode this week is, in its own way, a story about risk quietly moving to wherever it's hardest to see. Private equity has pushed private credit into life insurers, where state guarantee funds only start paying after the collapse. Iran's economy has been captured by a loyalty network that treats a Hezbollah fighter as more deserving of state resources than a Tehran professor. Branko Milanovic argues neoliberalism itself is giving way to a more mercantilist order, with Western middle classes and Chinese state-elites both quietly losing ground to new winners. Even the AI boom reads this way — CoreWeave and OpenAI are racing to buy compute at a scale "nobody thought was rational," while a survey of existential-risk workers finds them absorbing chronic guilt and hypervigilance that no institution is pricing in. The thread: when the visible manager of a risk steps back, someone still has to carry it — a bagholder, a policyholder, a burned-out researcher — and this week's episodes keep finding out who.

### Odd Lots — "Why Private Credit Got Entangled With Insurance"

**Guest:** Andrew Granato (Assistant Professor of Law, UT Austin Law School) and Pranjal Drall (JD-PhD Candidate in Financial Economics, Yale University; co-authors of "Private Credit's State Backstop: How Private Equity Socializes Risk Through Insurers") · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-07-31 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/why-private-credit-got-entangled-with-insurance)

**Thesis:** Private credit grew after 2008 as policymakers pushed risky lending out of the regulated banking system, but that risk has increasingly migrated into insurers — private equity now controls roughly $750 billion of life insurance assets and has pushed those insurers into affiliated, less-transparent private credit. Because state insurance guarantee funds are unfunded until after an insolvency, and because most states let failed insurers offset the resulting assessment with a full tax credit, Granato and Drall argue this amounts to a "stealth taxpayer bailout" that is structurally worse than FDIC deposit insurance — no pre-funded pool, no federal backstop, and coverage caps (around $200K, roughly the 40th percentile of policies) that leave many policyholders exposed.

- **The PE "flywheel."** Drall describes a PE firm with a buyout arm, a private credit fund, and a life insurer as capturing "the best of all three worlds" — long-dated, arguably permanent insurance capital funds illiquid private credit loans issued to the firm's own portfolio companies.
- **Scale of the shift.** Granato cites "recent estimates" of roughly $750 billion in life insurance assets now within private equity's purview, with insurer portfolios shifting away from formerly "stodgy triple-A rated AT&T bond portfolios."
- **Opaque valuations.** Private credit loans held by insurers are rated by third parties (via "private letter ratings") reported to the NAIC, but Granato says these ratings aren't publicly visible or auditable, and "new empirical literature... continually find[s] over valuation" in these assets.
- **Guarantee funds vs. FDIC.** Unlike the pre-funded, risk-weighted FDIC system, state guarantee funds only assess surviving insurers after a failure — the failed insurer "contributed zero dollars" — and in roughly 34 states that assessment is fully offset by a tax credit over five years, which Granato calls "economically equivalent to a taxpayer bailout... nobody ever votes on."
- **Perverse incentives.** Because assessments are based on premium volume rather than risk, Drall notes a conservative and a reckless insurer with equal premiums face the same backstop cost, creating "this kind of implicit subsidy" for riskier behavior — and, unlike FDIC-insured banks, insurers face no rate cap discouraging distressed firms from chasing yield.
- **Shadow reinsurance.** Granato explains that PE-backed insurers often cede assets and liabilities to captive reinsurers in low-tax jurisdictions like Bermuda, Iowa, or Vermont, after which "you lose all visibility into what's going on," even though NAIC data on the primary insurer is otherwise granular to the CUSIP level.
- **The Guggenheim/Mark Walter tie-in.** Alloway notes federal prosecutors are investigating Mark Walter (Guggenheim, owner of the LA Dodgers), whose affiliated insurers Delaware Life and Clear Spring revised disclosed affiliated-asset percentages from roughly 3-5% up to 40% after scrutiny.
- **Proposed fixes.** Granato suggests a Pigouvian capital surcharge on hard-to-value assets, ending guarantee-fund tax credits and pre-funding them like the FDIC, and importing banking's "source of strength doctrine" to make insurance holding-group affiliates share insolvency costs.

> "It is a stealth taxpayer bailout." — Andrew Granato

> "You have this widely dispersed retail base of policy holders, a large fraction of whom are totally insured, and so even you can do whatever you want and like in theory, they shouldn't care because no matter what, they have full coverage." — Andrew Granato

> "Under additional scrutiny, it moved from three to forty percent." — Joe Weisenthal

### Odd Lots — "How the Iranian Economy Actually Works"

**Guest:** Yeganeh Torbati (Iran correspondent, The New York Times) and Bozorgmehr Sharafedin (Head of Digital, Iran International; co-authors of *Stolen Revolution: Betrayal and Hope in Modern Iran*) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-07-30 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/how-the-iranian-economy-actually-works)

**Thesis:** Torbati and Sharafedin, who spent roughly five and a half years reporting their book, argue Iran evolved from an authoritarian Islamic Republic into a "mafia state" where access to resources is allocated by factional loyalty rather than citizenship — a Hezbollah member in Lebanon or a Houthi militia fighter is treated as more deserving of state largesse than a Tehran University professor. Bonyads (religious foundations) and the Revolutionary Guards captured whole sectors — the Guards reportedly now sell roughly half of Iran's oil exports — while a once-thriving, sanctions-protected startup scene (Digikala, Snap) was gradually absorbed by security-linked shareholders. The authors conclude the regime has never treated the economy as an end in itself, prioritizing ideological survival even when officials urge accommodation with the US.

- **Mafia-state mechanics.** Sharafedin defines it against normal totalitarianism: "you should imagine a layered society and citizens are rewarded based on their loyalty to the system," so a proxy militia fighter outranks a domestic professor, and the state funds foreign proxies even when it "can't afford to provide free milk at schools."
- **Bonyads and "invisible ports."** Mehdi Karroubi, running a war-veterans' foundation in the 1980s, got parliament to let it import goods without government audit or taxation; decades later, as Speaker of Parliament, he discovered the Revolutionary Guards had built dedicated southern ports that imported anything duty-free and unsupervised.
- **The 2004 airport shutdown.** When Iran opened a new international airport meant to rival Dubai, Revolutionary Guards drove armored land cruisers onto the runway to block landings because they'd lost the operating contract to a Turkish-Austrian firm; a second plane was diverted after a fighter jet was scrambled, and the airport was closed for months before reopening under state control.
- **Startup capture.** Iran was the second Middle Eastern country online after Israel; sanctions kept out Amazon, Uber and Lyft, letting local clones (Digikala, ride-hail app Snap) serve an ~80-85 million-person market. A backlash followed — Telegram smear campaigns, interrogations, office raids — until middlemen offered protection for roughly 5% of shares; Snap sold a large stake to a bonyad, and Digikala eventually sold 40% to a telecom part-owned by one.
- **Vaccine self-dealing.** During COVID, the Supreme Leader barred Western vaccine imports while awarding Iran's domestic vaccine contract to Setad, the conglomerate he controls — "the ideology and the self interest kind of really melding seamlessly," per Torbati.
- **Outlook.** Sharafedin notes Iran's inflation is "almost three figures" and its casualty tolerance vastly exceeds Washington's; Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf has said Iran wants investment mainly "from China," and both authors see no evidence the regime will subordinate ideology to economic reform.

> "So the more loyal you are to the government and the state, the more you will benefit from the resources." — Bozorgmehr Sharafedin

> "I think when you compare the casualties for the US administration, the death of eighteen soldiers is a lot, is significant, while for the Islamic Republic, the killing, the death of hundreds or thousands of soldiers is something that can easily absorb." — Bozorgmehr Sharafedin

> "For the Islamic Republic, the economy has never been an end goal in and of itself." — Yeganeh Torbati

### Odd Lots — "Branko Milanovic on What Comes After Globalization"

**Guest:** Branko Milanovic (research professor, Graduate Center at CUNY; author of *The Great Global Transformation: The United States, China, and the Remaking of the World Economic Order*) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-07-27 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/branko-milanovic-on-what-comes-after-globalization)

**Thesis:** Milanovic argues globalization's neoliberal era — which he traces back to 1974's New International Economic Order — is expiring, replaced by what he calls "national market liberalism": a mercantilist rupture of *international* free-trade rules while domestic policy (deregulation, low redistribution) stays essentially neoliberal. He roots this in his "elephant chart" work on global inequality, showing that a bottom-decile Italian who ranked at the 70th global percentile thirty years ago is now around the 55th, even as China's consumption-to-GDP ratio (roughly 40-45%, versus 70% in the US and 50% in India) reflects a deliberate political-economy choice to keep state resources available for projects like AI, railroads, and space exploration.

- **A new economic order, named for the first time.** Milanovic says his book is effectively "an autobiography" spanning four orders he's lived through, culminating in national market liberalism — where activities are "brought back home" and external policy turns "mercantilistic," while domestic policy under Trump, Macron, and others still "continu[es] with neoliberal policies."
- **The elephant chart's real message.** He revisits his famous chart to explain why Western middle classes "have not been doing well" (per-capita growth of just 1%) while the top decile boomed and China surged — illustrated by the anecdote of an Italian in the bottom decile falling from the 70th to the 55th global percentile, no longer able to afford a World Cup ticket to watch their own team.
- **China's elite has flipped from state to private.** Using survey data on the top 5% of Chinese urban earners, Milanovic notes that in 1988 two-thirds of elite income came from state enterprises, government, and Communist Party jobs; by 2023 that ratio reversed, with two-thirds now from private-sector jobs — a shift he compares to England's capitalist class growth from 1688-1900, except China compressed it into forty years versus England's 150.
- **"Homo plutia" and America's "foxy elites."** He describes a genuinely new class — the US top 10% (~30 million people) who rank in the top decile for both labor income and capital income simultaneously — and coins "foxy elites" for Americans who downplay their elite status while still enjoying it.
- **A "pedagogical tax" on extraordinary wealth.** Rather than a technocratic revenue tool, Milanovic frames a wealth tax as a political message: "You are not going to rule us... I'm going to have an extraordinary tax on you every year, and it is a pedagogical tax," aligning it with, though less extreme than, Piketty's proposals.
- **The Xi/Trump comparison, defended.** Pushed on lumping Trump, Xi, and Putin together as anti-elite figures, Milanovic clarifies he means they each came to power on backlash from populations "unhappy with what they have seen" — while Xi's move against Jack Ma's Ant IPO was a deliberate signal that Chinese capitalists get rich but don't get political influence.

> "I'm going to have an extraordinary tax on you every year, and it is a pedagogical tax." — Branko Milanovic

> "So long as China does not have that its own self power I think will be limited." — Branko Milanovic

### Odd Lots — "How Financial Advisors Can Grow During the Great Wealth Transfer" *(Sponsored Content)*

**Guest:** David Blanchett (Prudential Financial; Head of Retirement Research and Portfolio Manager at PGIM), Brittney Castro, CFP, AAMS, CRPC, and Chelsea Ransom-Cooper (Zenith Wealth Partners; Co-Founder and Chief Financial Planning Officer) · **Hosts:** Maggie Lake · **Aired:** 2026-07-26 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim. Sponsored content, produced and paid for by Bloomberg Media Studios and Prudential Financial.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/how-financial-advisors-can-grow-during-the-great-wealth-transfer-sponsored-content)

**Thesis:** More than $100 trillion is projected to change hands over the next 25 years (per Cerulli research cited by Prudential), but only about 19% of heirs say they'll stay with their parents' financial advisor. The panel argues this "Great Wealth Transfer" is really a retention crisis driven by an emotional and communication gap — advisors talk portfolios and data while younger, first-generation-wealth clients want empathy, plain language, and to be treated as individuals rather than inheritors of their parents' relationship.

- **The retention numbers are stark.** Blanchett cites Prudential's Cerulli-based research: over $100 trillion transferring across generations in the next 25 years, yet only "nineteen ish percent" of next-gen heirs plan to keep their parents' advisor.
- **Clients feel unheard, not underserved.** Castro says younger clients often view inherited advisors as an "old dinosaur" who isn't relating to their goals or lifestyle, driving them to leave even when the technical relationship was "successful."
- **A real-world defection.** Ransom-Cooper describes a family whose children met their parents' advisor and walked out saying, essentially, this isn't our person — then found a millennial-relatable advisor on their own.
- **Documented "protection" and "time-use" gaps.** Per an Alliance for Lifetime Income study, 62% of advisors think they discuss protection with clients but only 27% of clients report that conversation happening; similarly, most advisors say they discuss how clients will spend retirement time, but only 29% of clients report having that talk.
- **A confidence-knowledge mismatch.** Prudential's pulse survey found roughly 90% of mass-affluent Americans believe they're on track for retirement, yet only about 40% have an advisor and roughly a third have an actual financial plan.

> "Only about nineteen ish percent say they're going to stay with their advisor" — David Blanchett

> "This is not our guy, this is not our person" — Chelsea Ransom-Cooper

> "I don't love the word retirement because I think as negative baggage. I like the word financial independence." — David Blanchett

### Odd Lots — "How CoreWeave Sees the Market for Compute Right Now"

**Guest:** Brannin McBee (CoreWeave; co-founder and chief development officer) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-06-08 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/how-coreweave-sees-the-market-for-compute-right-now)

**Thesis:** Three years after CoreWeave was chip-constrained and pre-IPO, co-founder Brannin McBee says demand for GPU compute remains "unrelenting," with inference now exceeding 50% of platform utilization and a customer base that has diversified well beyond hyperscalers and AI labs — financial-services clients like Jane Street now contract directly, with that segment's backlog "approaching ten billion" dollars. The real constraint today isn't chips or land but "powered shell" capacity (energized, cooled data-center space) and its dependent supply chains — batteries, transformers, and electricians requiring a five-year-plus apprenticeship. Contract durations have stretched from 3-year deals two years ago to 5-year, non-cancellable commitments now, financed through $21 billion raised year-to-date.

- **Customer base has diversified sharply since the IPO.** McBee says CoreWeave now has "ten over one billion dollar clients," nine of the top ten AI labs, and a financial-services backlog "approaching ten billion" — including Jane Street, which contracts with CoreWeave directly rather than through an AI lab.
- **Inference has overtaken training as the dominant workload.** Inference "represent[s] well in access of fifty percent of infrastructure utilization" on the same GPU fleet used for training, with demand showing no sign of pulling back despite CFO "sticker shock" headlines.
- **Contract length has kept extending.** Two years ago CoreWeave signed 3-year take-or-pay deals; last year, 4-year; now clients "want it for five years" with fixed economics and no ability to upgrade, cancel, or interrupt.
- **The bottleneck has moved from chips to "powered shell."** McBee: land isn't the constraint — an energized, cooled data-center shell is, gated by batteries, transformers, and electrician-accreditation supply chains that "you can't really scale efficiently."
- **Financing has transformed the business.** CoreWeave has raised "over twenty one billion dollars" year-to-date and operates "over a gigawatt" of active power, via financing including the first investment-grade-rated, non-recourse HPC infrastructure deal.
- **Compute isn't fungible yet.** McBee argues an H100 in one cloud doesn't match the "goodput" of an H100 elsewhere, undercutting the idea of GPU compute becoming a liquid, tradable commodity soon.

> "I think we have ten over one billion dollar clients at this point, and our financial services client backlog is into tens of billions of dollars at this point." — Brannin McBee

> "Having a powered shell is the [bottleneck]... today." — Brannin McBee

> "The reality is this stuff isn't getting easier to operate... until it starts becoming easier, you don't really have a path to commoditization." — Brannin McBee

## BURNOUT & THE SUSTAINABLE SELF

### 80,000 Hours Podcast — "Spencer Greenberg on staying sane while trying to save the world"

**Guest:** Spencer Greenberg (founder and CEO of clearerthinking.org; coauthor of *The 12 Levers: The Complete Psychological Toolkit For Improving Your Life*) · **Hosts:** Luisa Rodriguez · **Aired:** 2026-07-28 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/spencer-greenberg-existential-risk-mental-health-12-levers/?utm_campaign=podcast__spencer-greenberg&utm_source=80000+Hours+Podcast&utm_medium=podcast)

**Thesis:** People working on existential risk, AI safety, and factory farming rarely hit full clinical depression or anxiety, but a survey of 60–110 such workers found a median of six psychological challenges each, with 68% struggling to switch off from work, 60% imposter syndrome, 59% burnout, 56% guilt, and 54% anxiety — and 45% had considered cutting back or leaving. Drawing on that survey, interviews with therapists, and Greenberg's new book, he and Rodriguez argue that chronic fear and guilt are bad fuel for sustained impact — hypervigilance is built for short bursts, not years of work, and the more effective path is treating rest as a genuine need, finding "clean fuel" motivation over "dirty fuel" self-flagellation, and accepting human limitations rather than denying them.

- **The survey quantifies the psychological toll.** Of 60–110 respondents (mostly aged 25–45, working under 10 years in the field), the median person reported six distinct psychological challenges; only 29% reported hopelessness/depression-adjacent feelings, and just 22% selected fear of death — lower than the "switching off" and imposter-syndrome numbers.
- **Doomscrolling is a broken coping mechanism, not useful exposure.** Greenberg uses a service that gives him one news story a day, reasoning "if it's not important enough to be the top news story of the day, it's probably not important enough to care about." He compares constant news-checking to a friend afraid of flying who obsessively checks weather reports: the brain doesn't learn "flying is safe," it learns "if I'm hypervigilant, it's safe."
- **"Clean fuel" vs. "dirty fuel" motivation.** Greenberg distinguishes self-flagellation ("dirty fuel," which works but carries burnout risk) from sustainable motivators like excitement, curiosity, or social collaboration ("clean fuel"), suggesting people experiment with alternative motivators on low-stakes tasks first.
- **The "12 Levers" came from a huge literature review.** Greenberg and coauthor Jeremy Stevenson studied over 100 popular self-help books and 20+ therapies, cataloguing almost 500 techniques, distilled into 12 high-level psychological strategies — surprising Greenberg, who expected to land around 50.
- **Certainty of doom is itself overconfidence.** Responding to Rodriguez's framing that she acts as if she's in the "tractable" possible worlds, Greenberg states plainly that being certain of failure is unjustified: "we don't need really high probabilities of success to feel hope or to have something meaningful to contribute."
- **Behavioural activation beats existential problem-solving for hopelessness.** For the minority who report hopelessness, Greenberg notes the most effective treatment isn't resolving the underlying existential question but simply resuming valued activities.

> "Failing to accept your limitations can actually be disastrous towards the goals that you care about." — Spencer Greenberg

> "If you're certain that we're doomed, then I would say you're overconfident." — Spencer Greenberg

> "It's like thinking that the way that you win a race is by going at maximum speed at every single moment. No, that's actually how you get injured or burn out." — Spencer Greenberg
