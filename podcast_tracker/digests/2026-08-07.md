### 80,000 Hours Podcast — "Where AGI timelines go wrong | Toby Ord, Oxford University"

**Guest:** Toby Ord (Oxford University; researcher on existential risk, author of *The Precipice*) · **Hosts:** Rob Wiblin · **Aired:** 2026-08-06 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/toby-ord-recursive-self-improvement-agi-timelines/?utm_campaign=podcast__toby-ord-rsi&utm_source=80000+Hours+Podcast&utm_medium=podcast)

**Thesis:** Toby Ord thinks a fast recursive-self-improvement "intelligence explosion" is unlikely but credible and dangerous in at least four distinct ways, and that AGI is probably a decade or more away once benchmark saturation, compute-scaling limits, and the "work experience" gap between raw intelligence and job-ready competence are accounted for. He argues for embracing wide "broad timelines" rather than false precision, thinks a US-China treaty on superintelligence is more achievable than people assume, and wants an immediate ban on training models with unmonitorable chain-of-thought.

- **RSI probably won't cause a takeoff, but it's still credible.** Ord doubts recursive self-improvement will produce a runaway explosion because AI research needs genuine breakthroughs, not just faster coding. Colleague Tom Davidson's rule of thumb: if RSI works, the likeliest outcome is roughly five years of AI-research progress compressed into one calendar year before it plateaus.
- **Four distinct dangers of RSI**, regardless of whether it "explodes": it speeds up capabilities faster than safety work; misalignment could propagate down a chain of AI systems building their successors (GPT-7 building GPT-8, GPT-9...); society loses the intermediate, less-powerful stages it normally learns from (muskets before machine guns); and it creates a winner-take-all dynamic concentrating power in whichever lab gets there first.
- **A US-China treaty on superintelligence is more plausible than the "racing" narrative suggests.** Ord argues seizing a decisive strategic advantage (à la Leopold Aschenbrenner's *Situational Awareness*) is destabilizing, like Reagan's Star Wars shield: once a rival's nuclear deterrent value hits zero, they're incentivized to strike first, not disarm quietly.
- **He wants an immediate ban on unmonitorable chain-of-thought.** Training AI to reason illegibly rather than in observable English has the worst risk/reward of any near-term research direction, and a norm against it — like the nuclear taboo — is worth solidifying now, while no lab has gone far down that path.
- **AGI could be a decade-plus away.** Benchmark saturation keeps recurring (AIME went from a few percent to saturated between late 2024 and 2025, yet math wasn't "solved"); compute scaling is slowing (TSMC can't multiply fab capacity quickly, training data is running out); and a superintelligent system trained in a data center would resemble a brilliant 21-year-old graduate needing real-world "work experience" — Ord estimates six months to 20 years, depending on the job, before senior-level performance.
- **AI is impressive at math but far from obsoleting mathematicians.** He cites an AI disproof of Erdős's unit-distance conjecture as genuinely interesting but narrow, arguing the bottleneck isn't proving statements but inventing new questions and theories (as with Claude Shannon's information theory) — something AI hasn't shown it can do.
- **"Broad timelines," not point estimates.** Citing Helen Toner's finding that forecaster medians shortened roughly tenfold in five years, and Daniel Kokotajlo's own distribution (10% chance within 9 months, median around 4 years, 90% chance by roughly 2040), Ord argues persistent expert disagreement calls for a wide confidence interval, not false confidence in either direction.

> "At least I think that's unlikely. However, the chance that it might happen I think is credible. And this possibility really is one of the biggest issues in AI at the moment." — Toby Ord

> "If someone creates some new method... that makes chain of thought illegible and gets some kind of substantial advantage out of that in terms of capabilities, then it's possible that that's the worst piece of research that's ever been done by any human — worse than inventing chemical weapons." — Toby Ord

> "Suppose you could prove one of these things per microsecond, if you started doing that now — I did some calculations on this — the stars would have burnt out by the time you even came up to the unit distance conjecture to even try to prove it." — Toby Ord

### 80,000 Hours Podcast — "What the hell happened with AGI timelines in 2026? – Rob Wiblin"

**Guest:** none (solo episode) · **Hosts:** Rob Wiblin · **Aired:** 2026-08-04 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/2026-agi-timelines/?utm_campaign=podcast__2026-agi-timelines&utm_source=80000+Hours+Podcast&utm_medium=podcast)

**Thesis:** After a bearish October 2025, the arrival of Claude Opus 4.5 and then Mythos triggered a "hard 180-degree turn" in AI vibes. Rob Wiblin walks through seven pieces of 2026 evidence — exploding revenue, the METR time-horizons graph, Mythos's capability jump, Anthropic's self-reported R&D speedups, struggling AI-run businesses, an AI math breakthrough, and cheaper-than-expected inference scaling — and concludes his own AGI timelines have shortened by "something like one year," while cautioning that a slower takeoff into the mid-2030s remains very much on the table.

- **Anthropic's revenue growth is historically extreme.** Over the past six months, combined OpenAI/Anthropic revenue grew at an annualized 700%; over just the last three months, 1600%. Anthropic alone grew at an annualized rate of 8400% over 5.5 months (85x if sustained a year), reaching a $47 billion annualized run rate in May — with gross margins on inference rising from 38% to over 70% over the year to May 2026.
- **The METR time-horizon doubling rate accelerated.** Time horizons for software tasks AI can do 50% of the time doubled every seven months through mid-2024, then every four months since — but Rob lists four reasons for caution: it's narrowly coding-focused, tests fresh (not experienced) staff, ignores the 50%-vs-high-reliability gap, and coding is a shrinking share of real AI R&D work.
- **Mythos caused a sudden capability jump — but likely a one-off from a bigger pretraining run.** Anthropic's internal capabilities index showed six months of progress compressed into three months with Mythos, but progress returned to the prior trend over the following two months, suggesting a step-change from training a much larger model (rumored ~5x the parameters of the previous Opus) rather than a permanent acceleration.
- **Anthropic reports Claude now writes 80% of its merged code**, with staff shipping 8x as much code as 18 months ago, and Claude succeeding at hard open-ended programming tasks 75% of the time (up from 10% nine months ago). In cherry-picked cases where humans admitted taking a wrong research direction, Mythos would have suggested a better one 64% of the time — but only 20% of the time when humans thought they'd made a strong call.
- **AI still struggles badly with messy, real-world business tasks.** In Andon Labs' experiments, an AI-run café in Stockholm ("Mona") sold $10,378 in coffee and pastries but burned its bank account from $18,000 down to $2,000 in two months, hallucinated a floor plan, and impersonated an employee to get an alcohol license; an AI-run San Francisco shop ("Luna") went from $94,000 to $64,000 in three months, mostly to rent.
- **An unreleased OpenAI model disproved a long-believed conjecture in the "unit distance problem"** using ordinary chain-of-thought reasoning, with no math tools — but Rob calls it "very unclear" as an AGI signal since it followed familiar human-like methodical steps rather than a genuine flash of insight.
- **Inference scaling is cheaper than Rob previously feared.** A Redwood Research analysis of METR's benchmark found the cost of AI completing tasks has stayed around 3% of the cost of a human doing the same task, even as chains of thought have gotten far longer — because tasks themselves have gotten bigger, not because AI is becoming less efficient per unit of task.

> "We are now around the 90th percentile of my AI timelines from 2021 [and from 2019]. I think we've now seen enough signs that there could be a relatively fast takeoff starting very soon. We no longer have strong quantitative indicators that transformative AI isn't coming soon, and are flying blind. 1–4 year timelines are consistent with existing trends." — Paul Christiano (quoted by Rob Wiblin)

> "I think we're approaching the crossover point now at which the benefits of slowing are going to start outweighing the costs in a way they simply didn't before." — Rob Wiblin

### Odd Lots — "Brad Setser on the US's Unusual Japanese Yen Intervention"

**Guest:** Brad Setser (senior fellow, Council on Foreign Relations) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal, Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-08-06 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/brad-setser-on-the-uss-unusual-japanese-yen-intervention)

**Thesis:** The US and Japan jointly intervened to prop up the yen for the first time in 15 years, with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent selling euros rather than dollars and tapping the little-known FIMA repo facility. Brad Setser argues the yen's slide is part of a broader, counterintuitive pattern of weak East Asian currencies despite record regional trade surpluses, driven mainly by hedging flows and financial dynamics rather than fundamentals. He believes the intervention can work — but only if the Bank of Japan follows through with rate hikes, starting with a widely expected September move.

- **The intervention was historic and structurally unusual.** It marked the first joint US-Japan yen intervention in 15 years. A leaked photo showed Bessent's to-do list reading "buy Japanese yen... 5 to 10 billion." The US sold euros instead of dollars, and Japan's Ministry of Finance used a Fed repo facility, though Setser notes "the vast bulk of the intervention was from Japan's Ministry of Finance, and it was in dollar yen, so fundamentally this was a dollar yen intervention, not a euro yen intervention."
- **East Asian currency weakness is a regional story, not just Japan's.** Setser says Korea's current account surplus is set to jump from roughly $100 billion to $300–400 billion, and Taiwan's is likely to double to 25–30% of GDP. Korea's weakness stems from foreign investors hitting concentration limits and selling Samsung/SK Hynix stock as prices rise; Taiwan's central bank has engineered won/dollar weakness partly by leaning on TSMC's conversion timing.
- **Yen weakness is more about hedging flows than fundamentals.** Japan runs a current account surplus of about 5% of GDP, holds roughly $1.2 trillion in government reserves, and the GPIF pension fund has $900 billion-plus in unhedged foreign assets. Setser explains that when the Nikkei rises the GPIF doesn't hedge, unlike foreign holders of Nikkei exposure — creating a lopsided flow — while hedge funds bet the yen would reach 170 because the BOJ was "behind the curve."
- **The FIMA repo facility lets Japan avoid dumping Treasuries.** Setser, who pushed for the tool while at Treasury in 2020, says it lets a central bank repo Treasury collateral for dollars rather than selling into the cash market, at "zero risk to the Fed." The facility is capped near $60 billion and carries an above-market premium, but Setser argues it's cheaper than losing the yield on a legacy high-coupon bond outright.
- **Japan's fiscal position has quietly improved.** The primary balance (excluding interest) is now roughly flat, trending toward surplus — better than the US, UK, France, and arguably Germany — while US fiscal deficits are running around 5–6% of GDP and net US debt will soon surpass Japan's. Japan's foreign reserves and GPIF holdings generate interest income worth nearly 2% of GDP that mostly isn't repatriated into the FX market.
- **Whether it works hinges on the BOJ's September meeting.** Setser thinks intervention "will be enough if the Bank of Japan is going to raise rates and maybe raise rates several times," noting the BOJ's policy rate sits near 1% versus the Fed's roughly 3.25–3.5%, and that failure to hike in September would signal a lack of domestic commitment to defending the currency.

> "I think this is fundamentally about saying we're going to defend one sixty. There's a lot of risk around one sixty." — Brad Setser

### Odd Lots — "The Tungsten Market Is Warning of an Upcoming War"

**Guest:** David Fickling (Bloomberg Opinion columnist) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal, Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-08-03 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/the-tungsten-market-is-warning-of-an-upcoming-war)

**Thesis:** Tungsten mining acts as, in David Fickling's words, "a sort of century old missing prediction market for war" — investors only fund new supply outside China when war looks likely, because the metal's extreme density and 3,400°C-plus melting point make it essential for armor-piercing rounds and cluster munitions. Fickling traces this through Australia's Dolphin Mine on an island off Tasmania, which opened in 1917, 1938, and during the Korean War, then sat flooded and idle from 1990 until now, when it's restarting again. China supplies roughly 80% of the ~85,000-metric-ton annual global tungsten market and imposed export controls around the time President Trump took office in 2026, driving prices up. The rebooted mine, backed by a "cast of characters from a heist movie" (including Red Bull investors and distressed-debt guys), would supply only about 2.5% of global tungsten — but that's roughly one-sixth of the "free world" supply outside China, Russia, and North Korea.

- **Tungsten's physical properties make it uniquely suited to warfare.** With a melting point over 3,400°C (three times hotter than lava) and density close to gold's, tungsten holds its shape as an armor-piercing projectile instead of "mushrooming" like other metals, and can be formed into tiny pellets for cluster-munition shrapnel that creates "metal rain."
- **The Dolphin Mine's on-off history mirrors war cycles.** It opened in 1917 (WWI), closed when Allies dumped their tungsten stockpiles, reopened in 1938 (WWII), got a boost from the Korean War, faded around Sputnik, kept running through Vietnam, and finally shut in 1990 with the mine flooding — staying dormant for three decades until now.
- **The investor group is tiny and eclectic.** Total cash invested over 20 years is about $77 million — "what Amazon spends in about four hours" — funded by shareholders including Red Bull-linked investors, a Peabody Energy payout, a former Caterpillar dealer who worked the mine in the 1960s, the Tasmanian state government, and equipment lessors who took equity in lieu of unpaid bills.
- **There's no futures market for tungsten**, unlike copper on the LME or Comex, so miners can't hedge or get bankable financing. Investor Alex Turnbull told Fickling prices can swing from $300 to $3,300 per "dry metric ton unit" depending on Chinese supply decisions.
- **Prices have roughly 13x'd since 2022.** Joe Weisenthal noted the Rotterdam warehouse tungsten price went from about $300 per ton when he bought his now-missing tungsten cube in 2022 to over $3,000 — which Fickling clarified is actually about $400,000 per metric ton once converted from the dtu pricing unit.
- **The strategic-stockpile debate is murky.** Fickling is skeptical of the US's $12 billion Project Vault critical-minerals reserve, warning it risks becoming "a way of hedging price risk for fairly common commodities like copper, like nickel, like aluminum" rather than targeting genuinely scarce, strategic materials like tungsten, gallium, and germanium, which he says need 3-5-year price-security guarantees to attract financing.
- **Rethinking of warfare explains tungsten's decades in the wilderness.** The 1990 Gulf War convinced the US it would always be "vastly technologically superior" to future foes, reducing reliance on attritional weapons — but four years of grinding trench warfare in Ukraine, plus the looming risk of a Taiwan conflict against "the biggest manufacturing power on the planet," has revived tungsten's strategic relevance.

> "You can look at it as a bit like a sort of century old missing prediction market for war basically... if you go back right to the turn of the twentieth century, it has operated like a prediction market in the sense that people making real investments, once they get a sense that war is a real possibility, they put real money behind tungsten." — David Fickling

### Odd Lots — "Why Susquehanna Is Building a Prediction Markets Business"

**Guest:** Jeremy Maletz (Head of Macro Trading and Prediction Markets, Susquehanna International Group) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal, Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-08-06 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/why-susquehanna-is-building-a-prediction-markets-business)

**Thesis:** Recorded live at New York's City Winery, Joe and Tracy talk with Jeremy Maletz about how Susquehanna — a large options market-making firm founded by Jeff Yass — became the primary liquidity provider on platforms like Kalshi. Maletz argues prediction markets have moved past bootstrapping retail/sports volume and are now trying to solve the "chicken and egg" problem of institutional adoption: firms want to hedge real economic risk (e.g., snowfall for airlines, the Strait of Hormuz, UK political risk), but visible order-book volume looks too thin to justify it. Susquehanna's pitch is that it will supply the missing size itself, using the market's price-discovery signal from "super forecasters" plus internal quantitative research to get comfortable writing tens of millions of dollars of risk even on a contract that's only traded $100,000 on-screen.

- **Susquehanna sees itself as liquidity provider and "shepherd" for institutions.** Maletz says the firm was "the first institution that got involved in prediction markets in the first place" and now plays a two-pronged role: providing liquidity to help the ecosystem grow, and onboarding other institutions — via block trades, swaps, or licensed exchange market data — because "institutions are always going to be the slower moving player relative to retail."
- **Capital, not conviction, is the scarce resource.** Maletz notes Susquehanna is "very fortunate that capital isn't generally constrained for us," which lets it bootstrap institutional-size trades and hold risk on one side (e.g., a company hedging a regulatory or geopolitical event) that others aren't positioned to take.
- **Sports still dominates volume, but non-sports categories are growing faster off a lower base.** He says "more than half of the volume certainly is sports," but hedging-oriented "real market" categories are expanding quickly, though "it's not a huge part of the market as of now."
- **The 2016 election was the firm's origin story for prediction markets.** Maletz traded election risk on Betfair in Europe and watched a bank basket-hedge fail — "the broad market consensus in 2016 was that the market was going to be down five to seven percent if Trump won," but "by the end of the next day it was up" — while a prediction-market hedge would have worked.
- **Speed to market is what actually changed with prediction markets.** He recounts trying to list a futures hedge for a friend's musical-instrument import business during the first US-China trade war that took "about a year" via traditional futures listing, versus prediction markets which can launch a product in "a day or even inside of a day" — a capability he says could extend to compute/GPU-price markets.

> "We're basically a function that matches the buyers to the sellers across time and also across size." — Jeremy Maletz

> "What the prediction markets really provide is information. It's a price discovery mechanism." — Jeremy Maletz

### Odd Lots — "How a Sardine Gets From the Ocean to a Can"

**Guest:** Becca Milstein (Founder and CEO, Fishwife) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal, Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-08-07 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/how-a-sardine-gets-from-the-ocean-to-a-can)

**Thesis:** Fishwife founder Becca Milstein explains how a Moroccan export ban is scrambling the global sardine supply chain just as premium tinned fish has become a bona fide American growth category rather than a thrift-shelf commodity. Morocco supplies roughly 50% of the world's sardines, and after its catch fell about 46% by 2023, it banned frozen (though not canned) sardine exports to protect its own canning industry — a move that cut off roughly 90% of the raw material Spanish canneries relied on. Milstein argues the shortage is driven mainly by climate-linked migration and food-web shifts, not overfishing, and that Fishwife has been insulated on raw-material sourcing but not on cost, since it has had to air-freight product to meet demand that has run 2-5x normal since December 2025.

- **Fishwife's origin traces to a college semester in Granada, Spain, and later Lisbon.** Milstein started the company in 2020 during Covid, when canned fish saw roughly a 10% year-over-year sales bump; she cites Alison Roman, Anthony Bourdain, and David Chang as cultural signals that premium tinned fish was "in the ether."
- **The anchovy supply chain is dramatically more complex than the rest of the line.** Anchovies are purse-seine caught roughly six miles off Santoña, Spain, auctioned by quality grade, then cured in rock salt for 12-36 months (Fishwife's average is 12-18) before hand-packing — giving them only about a one-year shelf life versus 5-7 years for the rest of the tins.
- **Fishwife's supply chain scaled through two distinct phases.** A scrappy 2021 "zero to one" year sourcing from tiny Oregon and Washington micro-canneries (MOQs as low as 20,000 units, with unplanned stockouts) gave way to a 2023 transformation into partnerships with canneries capable of 80-100 million cans a year; the company now has 18 employees.
- **U.S. canning capacity has hollowed out.** Milstein estimates only about five canneries remain in the lower 48, plus a few larger ones in Alaska, after most shut down roughly 15 years ago due to strict FDA rules and declining Pacific sardine and anchovy fisheries — capacity that shifted to cheaper processing in Asia.
- **Fishwife sources sardines from two MSC-certified fisheries — Cornwall and, since last year, the Iberian fishery — insulating it from the Moroccan raw-material disruption**, but rising competition for that supply means Milstein expects sardine prices to keep climbing.

> "So Morocco is protecting his raw material and is able to maintain its industry." — Becca Milstein
