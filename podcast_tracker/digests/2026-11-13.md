## CHIPS, SPIES AND LEAKY CONTROLS

### Odd Lots — "What Nvidia's Megaspeed Scandal Is Really All About"

**Guest:** Mackenzie Hawkins (Bloomberg News tech reporter, Hong Kong; covers chip geopolitics and smuggling) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal, Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-11-12 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim (auto-transcript garbles some proper nouns, e.g. "Jensen Wong").* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/what-nvidias-megaspeed-scandal-is-really-all-about)

**Thesis:** Hawkins says the scandal is new but the story is old. Megaspeed is the largest Southeast Asian buyer of Nvidia chips and a major compute supplier to Alibaba. It was spun off from a Chinese gaming company, passed through a BVI shell, incorporated in Singapore, and became an official Nvidia Cloud Partner four days later. US and Singapore investigators had been looking at it for at least a year. The real issue, she says, is that US export controls don't cover rented compute, and enforcement depends on a few officials trusting Nvidia's self-reporting.

- **Roughly 50,000 chips were unaccounted for.** Nvidia checked Megaspeed's chips across more than 12 data centres owned by eight companies in three countries, at the US government's request. When Bloomberg compared Nvidia's count with Malaysian and Indonesian trade records, about 50,000 chips were missing, most of them Blackwells. Nvidia then said it had found warehouses holding "substantially all" of them and promised more checks. Hawkins has asked 10 to 12 times since whether those checks happened. Megaspeed's largest data centre has since dropped it as a tenant, and two other operators contradicted Nvidia's account on the record.
- **An investor deck pointed to Shanghai.** A 2024 deck listed five data centres in three countries. The one in an unnamed third country, the only one described as outside Southeast Asia, was illustrated with a rendering of a large Shanghai project. Megaspeed's Chinese "corporate twin" has been hiring engineers there to work on restricted Nvidia hardware. Megaspeed denies any smuggling, and Nvidia says it found no evidence of it.
- **How smuggling actually works.** It is rarely couriers with backpacks. Buyers place orders with a stated destination outside China and plan from the start to divert them. Sometimes the stated data centre doesn't exist. One defendant who pleaded guilty had declared restricted Nvidia hardware as "adapter modules." The Supermicro co-founder indictment alleges $2.5 billion of hardware went to a Thai intermediary that never had the data centres to install it.
- **Nvidia's general counsel contacted enforcement directly.** Tim Teeter called the head of BIS about Alice Huang's detention in Singapore, then emailed staff working on the case. Afterwards, agents had to route any information request to Nvidia through Jeffrey Kessler, a rule that applies to no other big tech company. Then the DOJ asked BIS to stop working the case. The US has about a dozen export-control officers overseas, rising to 25.
- **Renting chips to China is mostly legal.** The Biden-era AI diffusion rule would have restricted it, but the Trump administration isn't enforcing it, and two later replacement rules were dropped. Michael Kratzios said Moonshot got Blackwell access through Thailand. BIS's enforcement unit has since started mapping where remote access happens, even though the practice isn't illegal.
- **Huawei is far behind but starting to export.** Huawei executives say it will take until 2030 to meet China's own demand. DeepSeek ordered 160,000 Huawei 950DTs for an Inner Mongolia cluster, and Huawei can't fill the order for more than a year. Its total output of those chips is in the low hundreds of thousands. Even so, Huawei bid on an Egyptian defence tender, about 2,000 chips, and a US consortium put together a counterbid.
- **Tracy's question.** If chips are as scarce as Nvidia says, it can afford to be picky about customers. So why take the risk?

### 80,000 Hours Podcast — "The US is building the smartest AI. China is running a different race"

**Presenter:** Luisa Rodriguez (80,000 Hours; solo narrated video essay, recorded November 4) · **Aired:** 2026-11-10 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/china-ai-race-chips-slowdown/)

**Thesis:** China's best models are about six months behind the US, even though the US has roughly 16 times China's AI computing power. Rodriguez says the gap is that small because China buys, smuggles and rents American chips and copies American models through distillation. So the size of the US lead is "in large part a policy choice." If "China will catch up" is the case against slowing down, the US should first stop giving so much away. A cold open notes that four days after recording, Huang admitted the affair with an alleged Chinese spy.

- **Most of China's compute is American.** By the end of next year about two-thirds of Chinese AI compute will run on American chips. This year Chinese firms bought more than a million Nvidia gaming cards that fall just under the export limits. One smuggling network moved, or tried to move, at least $160 million of chips. The biggest channel is renting. Tencent has a deal to rent from Oracle, ByteDance runs a cluster in Norway, and four engineers flew to Malaysia with suitcases holding 15 hard drives each to train on 300 rented servers.
- **Enforcement can work.** When Washington threatened restrictions, Malaysia began requiring export permits, and Chinese server imports from Malaysia and Singapore fell about 80%.
- **Distillation closes about half the gap.** On compute alone, China should be about 13 months behind. The Institute for Progress estimates copying makes up about half the difference, the equivalent of four times as many chips. Anthropic says Alibaba exchanged more than 151 million messages with Claude in three months, and one campaign by DeepSeek, Moonshot and MiniMax used about 24,000 fake accounts. Anthropic says it cut harvestable data by 90–99%. Copiers then move to whichever lab has the weakest defences.
- **China is running a different race.** Alibaba's Lin Junyang gave China less than a 20% chance of overtaking US labs in three to five years and called that "highly optimistic." Zhipu's Tang Jie said the gap may be widening. Beijing's real aim is AI deployed everywhere, and there China has big advantages. It installed about 354,000 industrial robots last year, 59% of the world's total. Chinese firms made 97% of humanoid robots sold in the first half of this year. China is adding nearly six times the US's generating capacity, while US data centres face a projected 44 GW shortfall by 2028.
- **Conflicts of interest.** Huang warns that China is "nanoseconds behind" while pushing to sell it more chips. David Sacks said "every month counts" while pushing to loosen restrictions, and the New York Times found he held stakes in 449 AI-related companies.
- **Security is the real hole.** Top US labs rate roughly 2–3 on a 5-level security scale. Level 5 means stopping the most capable state hackers. Former Google engineer Linwei Ding was convicted in January of stealing AI supercomputer secrets. A stolen model loses its value as newer ones arrive, but stolen training techniques could help China permanently.

> "So if you care about competing with China, the first step isn't going faster. It's to stop giving so much away." — Luisa Rodriguez

> "Because right now, China is renting American chips." — Luisa Rodriguez

### 80,000 Hours Podcast — "Is Anthropic on the cusp of RSI? Inside the redacted METR report"

**Presenter:** Luisa Rodriguez (80,000 Hours; solo narrated video essay, recorded November 9) · **Aired:** 2026-11-12 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/metr-anthropic-recursive-self-improvement-RSI/)

**Thesis:** METR spent 11 weeks inside Anthropic with access to internal evaluations, training data and staff interviews. It concluded that as of October, Anthropic was improving its AI about twice as fast as a year earlier, with a plausible range of 1.3x to 3x. Its best guess is that about 80% of the speed-up comes from Anthropic's AI doing its own research. METR found no bottleneck likely to slow this within a year and says the speed-up itself may be increasing. Rodriguez argues this is the start of recursive self-improvement, and that voluntary, company-controlled audits can't handle it.

- **The evidence.** Plotted over time, Anthropic's test scores, including three unreleased internal models, become about twice as steep from around October 2025. That is roughly when Claude started coding autonomously for hours. Claude went from doing almost none of the research work end to end early this year to about a quarter by August, under human supervision. METR ruled out more compute as the cause, and a hiring spree, new data or a single breakthrough can't explain how long the speed-up has lasted.
- **Anthropic's own threshold.** Anthropic's line is "two years of progress in one." Its August report said progress had sped up "by less than a factor of 2," but METR's run of the private numbers gave 1.97. Anthropic's latest IPO filing now says it has crossed the line and is "evaluating the implications."
- **The pushback.** Toby Ord argues Mythos was a one-off jump from a bigger training run, and allowing for that gives about 1.4x. Buck Shlegeris replies that the test scores were the only unredacted part of the evidence, and METR says the redacted material strengthened its conclusion. Economist Karthik Tadepalli set out to cast doubt on the result and ended up convinced. The biggest caveat is that all of this is measured on easy-to-grade tasks.
- **Experts changed their minds.** Ryan Greenblatt says he was "seriously wrong" and now expects superintelligence within one to two years. Shlegeris puts the chance of superintelligence within the next year at about one in three, and expects AIs to take over if it happens. Rodriguez notes that the AI 2027 scenario didn't expect this kind of speed-up until early 2027.
- **Anthropic set the terms.** It chose what METR saw and what went into the public report, and it tightened its redaction rules in the final week. METR worried that being too critical would cost it future access. Rodriguez wants every frontier lab required to report how much AI is speeding up its own research, with independent checks. She backs Alex Bores's call for Congress to ban fully automating AI research, an idea with supporters from Bernie Sanders to Steve Bannon.

> "I just got through reading what may be the scariest AI report I’ve ever seen." — Luisa Rodriguez

> "That’s what happens when something keeps speeding up. It looks manageable right up until it isn’t." — Luisa Rodriguez

### Odd Lots — "Why a $2 Billion Law Firm Suddenly Collapsed"

**Guest:** John Morley (professor, Yale Law School; author of the 2015 paper "Why Law Firms Collapse") · **Hosts:** Tracy Alloway, Joe Weisenthal (recorded November 10) · **Aired:** 2026-11-13 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/why-a-2-billion-law-firm-suddenly-collapsed)

**Thesis:** Morley's 2015 paper predicted that a law firm could collapse with almost no debt and rising profits, as long as profits were rising more slowly than its peers'. That is what happened to Weil Gotshal. Its rank in profits per partner fell from 9th in 2020 to about 20th in 2025. When rainmakers leave, revenue falls faster than costs, so pay for the remaining partners falls. The risk of clawback grows, and practices that depended on the departed partners stop being viable. The result is a "partner run," the law-firm version of a bank run.

- **Partners as depositors.** In bankruptcy, profit distributions to partners count as discretionary payments, not wages. Payments made while the firm was insolvent can be clawed back as fraudulent transfers. Morley's example: a Weil partner in September who sees a 50% chance of collapse should value $2 million of expected pay at $1 million. That makes a $1.5 million offer from Gibson Dunn a raise, not a cut.
- **Stuck in the middle.** Big Law is splitting between huge platforms like Kirkland and Latham and a small elite like Wachtell and Cravath. Weil stayed mid-sized on purpose. Rainmaker Mike Aiello's move to Cravath in September was "the final straw." Morley thought the end was inevitable when Ramona Nee declined to become chair and moved to Kirkland.
- **Why investors can't fix it.** ABA Model Rule 5.6 bans lawyer non-competes, so a private-equity buyer could pay out the partners and watch them walk out the next day. Investors use managed-service organisations (MSOs) instead, and lawyers' MSO equity is forfeited if they leave. Only Arizona and Utah allow non-lawyer ownership.
- **The counterexample.** Slater & Gordon was a listed Australian firm that overpaid for Quindell, whose books had been cooked. Its equity was wiped out and its junior debt converted to equity, and it carried on. Lawyers who are employees rather than owners have no reason to run.
- **What the wind-down costs.** Weil closed quickly, which limits clawback exposure. But landlords can claim the full remaining term of their leases, and that will consume the cash. Partners will lose the January payout, typically 50–60% of a year's pay, after working 10 months for it.
- **Partners don't invest for the future, AI included.** A partner's equity disappears when they retire, so a 61-year-old partner has little reason to fund what pays off in ten years. Kirkland's announced $500 million AI investment over five years is small next to roughly $80 billion of revenue over that period.

> "A law firm collapse is kind of like a bank run. It's a crisis of confidence." — John Morley

> "Law firms will renovate AI. They won't buy or develop AI. They will rent it." — John Morley

## CITIES, GENERATIONS AND INNER LIVES

### Odd Lots — "This Is How New York City Takes Out Its Trash"

**Guest:** Joshua Goodman (deputy commissioner for public affairs and customer experience, NYC Department of Sanitation) · **Hosts:** Tracy Alloway, Joe Weisenthal · **Aired:** 2026-11-09 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/this-is-how-new-york-city-takes-out-its-trash)

**Thesis:** DSNY handles 24 million pounds of trash, recycling and compost every day, which Goodman calls "the definitional Sisyphean task." He describes it as the last free, unlimited city service, with no sign-up and no means test. He says LaGuardia's line that there's no partisan way to pick up garbage is "patently untrue." Who holds office determines whether a city takes on a 50-year-old problem, and New York is finally moving its trash bags off the sidewalks.

- **Why New York has black bags.** The 1811 street grid left the city without alleys. After the 1968 sanitation strike, Mayor Lindsay's "Committee on the Black Bag" adopted plastic bags, which the plastics industry promoted as rodent-resistant. They weren't.
- **Where your trash goes.** The 12–13 ton trucks run routes designed to fill up within a single shift. Since the Staten Island landfill closed in 2000, five transfer stations (a multi-billion-dollar, 15-year project) seal waste into containers that leave by barge or rail. Paper goes to Staten Island, where it may become tomorrow's pizza box. DSNY gave away about 15 million pounds of finished compost this year.
- **"Recycling is a scam" is wrong, at least in NYC.** Long-term contracts guarantee that properly sorted material is reused. Keeping paper separate from metal, glass and plastic protected the city when China's 2017–18 policy change wrecked other cities' recycling markets.
- **Containerisation, built from data.** DSNY did a volumetric analysis of 70,000 blocks, converting a century of weight data into volume. Buildings with 1–9 units use wheeled bins. Buildings with 31 or more get their own on-street "Empire bin," and those with 10–30 choose. The service started in West Harlem in June 2025, reaches Brooklyn CD2 this fall, and is funded for the whole city by the end of 2031. It will take about 30,000 of the city's 3 million parking spaces, not the 150,000 first estimated.
- **DSNY designed its own truck.** No suitable automated side-loader existed, and industry said designing one would take five years. DSNY combined a European body with a Mack chassis and had a prototype in about 10 months. About 20 of its roughly 2,300 trucks are now running. Half of sanitation workers' line-of-duty injuries are sprains and strains from throwing bags. Needle injuries are also rising, which DSNY suspects comes from GLP-1s bought online.
- **Defensible space.** Bins are assigned to buildings rather than shared, based on Oscar Newman's finding that spaces belonging to no one get neglected. Barcelona's lesson: don't switch the whole fleet to side-loaders, because people still leave couches out.

> "If you properly sort a can or bottle in New York City, it will become a new can or bottle or other reusable item." — Joshua Goodman

> "There will never be a time when DSNY says, great news, everyone." — Joshua Goodman
