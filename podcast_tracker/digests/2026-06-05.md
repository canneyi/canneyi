### Odd Lots — Inside Hudson River Trading's Blistering Token Burn
*Published 2026-06-05 · 31 min · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/inside-hudson-river-tradings-blistering-token-burn)*

**Guest / Format:** Interview — Iain Dunning (head of AI, Hudson River Trading) with Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway; recorded at Odd Lots live show, City Winery, New York (350 attendees).

**One-sentence framing:** A follow-up on how one of the world's largest market makers is actually deploying AI seven months on — focusing on compute costs, bottlenecks, chip strategy, and the unusual psychology of running powerful models in a trading shop.

**Topics**
- **Could HRT launch an LLM?**
  - Dunning conceded HRT has the training compute and the research discipline to potentially build something comparable to DeepSeek, but characterised reaching the true frontier as "a hugely capital-intensive task" they wouldn't claim to be able to do
- **Token burn and cost of memory**
  - The episode's central thread: how many tokens HRT employees actually consume, what memory costs, and where the real bottlenecks sit in inference pipelines
- **Chip strategy**
  - HRT is contemplating developing its own chips — a signal that at sufficient scale the economics of custom silicon vs. third-party GPUs tips even for non-hyperscalers
- **AI-induced delirium**
  - Discussed as a real phenomenon: engineers pushing models hard enough that outputs become unreliable in ways that feel almost hallucinatory at scale

**Notable claims / arguments**
- HRT's research cycle — rapid iteration, strong feedback loops — is structurally well-suited to training models, even if frontier scale is a separate challenge

**Quotes**
> "I think we're good at training models. We have a lot of compute and people are good at doing the cycle of research which is required to catch up to the sort of frontier. However, reaching the frontier is clearly a very daunting task." — Iain Dunning

**Open questions raised**
- At what scale does it become economically rational for a non-hyperscaler to develop proprietary silicon?
- How should trading firms think about AI memory cost as a new line item alongside compute?

- Grief work is intrinsic to pilgrimage: you cannot move forward without putting down something

**Frameworks / mental models introduced**
- **Ecology of practices** — the idea that one's habits, rituals, and relationships form a mutually supporting system that can only be tested holistically, not piecemeal

### Dwarkesh Podcast — Alex Imas and Phil Trammell – What remains scarce after AGI?
*Published 2026-06-04 · 1 hr 16 min · [Listen](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/alex-imas-phil-trammell)*

**Guest / Format:** Roundtable — Dwarkesh Patel with Alex Imas (Director of AGI Economics, Google DeepMind; Professor of Economics, University of Chicago) and Phil Trammell (Head of Economics, Epoch; research scholar, Stanford).

**One-sentence framing:** Two economists explore what economic theory actually predicts in a world of radical automation — what stays scarce, whether inequality must explode, and how to tax and distribute AI-generated wealth.

**Topics**
- **What will be scarce?**
  - Alex Imas: the "relational sector" — goods and services where human involvement is part of the value (ballerinas, baristas, therapists) — will retain scarcity because humans themselves are finite
  - But Dwarkesh presses: if the machine economy is mostly a closed loop not requiring human labour, won't the human economy become a diminishing share of total output?
- **The machine economy problem**
  - If AI can do everything, only humans with preferences for human-produced goods sustain a human labour market — but some wealth from that market flows out to purchase automated goods, making it an open, shrinking system
  - Imas pushes back by noting that economists' individual forecasts have been "famously terrible" and argues for prediction markets over expert forecasts
- **Ricardo's 200-year-old debate**
  - Ricardo, at the start of the Industrial Revolution, predicted mass unemployment from mechanisation — he was right that those specific jobs disappeared, but spectacularly wrong about the end state (near-full employment persisted)
  - The lesson for AI: automation destroys categories of work while creating new demand; the question is whether the adjustment is fast enough and who bears the cost
- **Optimal taxation and redistribution**
  - How should AI wealth be taxed? The conversation covers the "messy middle" scenario (partial automation over years, not sudden AGI), why demand collapse is unlikely, and why developing countries face a distinctive challenge in indexing into AI gains
- **Human employees in a machine economy**
  - Even in highly automated firms, integrating human employees raises coordination costs; the transcript notes "00:39:26 – Human employees would be hard to integrate into the machine economy" as a distinct challenge

**Notable claims / arguments**
- Prediction markets over expert forecasts for macro AI scenarios — the track record of individual economists is too poor to justify confidence
- Whether some humans or AIs intrinsically value wealth accumulation (and not just consumption) changes the dynamics of how inequality evolves

**Frameworks / mental models introduced**
- **Relational sector** — the subset of economic output where human participation is constitutive of the good's value, not merely instrumental
- **Messy Middle** — a partial-automation scenario where AI augments but doesn't replace most labour for a prolonged transitional period

**References (books, papers, people, prior episodes)**
- Andrey Fradkin, Brian Jabarian, and Andrew Koh — blog post on economists' forecasts about the labour market, finding high disagreement and advocating for prediction markets
- David Ricardo — *Principles of Political Economy* — the classical economist who simultaneously predicted industrial prosperity and mass unemployment, and was right about the former, wrong about the latter

**Quotes**
> "One robot now turns into many robots next year, but the number of ballerinas is the same." — Dwarkesh Patel (framing the scarcity of human performance)

**Open questions raised**
- Is there a taxation instrument that can redistribute AI wealth without destroying the incentive to develop AI in the first place?
- For countries outside the AI supply chain, what is the optimal strategy to capture some of the gains?

*Published 2026-06-04 · 17 min · [Listen](https://soundcloud.com/epsilontheory/notes-on-notes-i-want-it-but-i)*

**Guest / Format:** Interview — Matt Zeigler and Brent Donnelly discussing Donnelly's essay "I Want It, But I Don't Like It."

**One-sentence framing:** A short, pointed conversation about smartphone and social-media addiction as a structural feature of modern attention capture — and what to do about it personally.

**Topics**
- **The "dark zone" of smartphone use**
  - Donnelly argues phone use produces a reward-seeking loop analogous to gambling addiction: you want to check, you do check, you don't feel satisfied, you want to check again — a hedonic treadmill that generates craving without pleasure
- **The value of boredom**
  - Boredom is rehabilitated as cognitively productive: it is the mental state in which the mind consolidates, wanders creatively, and recovers attention capacity; smartphones eliminate it
- **Writing without AI**
  - Donnelly discusses how he structures his writing process specifically to protect his own voice from AI homogenisation — he won't let tools write for him, even for efficiency

**Notable claims / arguments**
- Social media and smartphones create "attention capture" that differs from ordinary distraction: it is engineered, persistent, and compounds over time in ways that ordinary entertainment does not

**Open questions raised**
- Can structural design solutions (app timers, greyscale displays) substitute for genuine behavioural change, or does the addiction logic route around them?

### Founders — #420 The Lost Years of Steve Jobs
*Published 2026-06-04 · 53 min · [Listen](N/A)*

**Guest / Format:** Solo — David Senra reads and synthesises *Steve Jobs in Exile: The Untold Story of NeXT and the Remaking of an American Visionary* by Geoffrey Cain.

**One-sentence framing:** The NeXT period — Jobs's decade in exile after being pushed out of Apple — is examined as the crucible that produced the older, stranger, and ultimately more effective founder who returned to rescue Apple.

**Topics**
- **The NeXT failure as education**
  - NeXT never achieved commercial success, yet it produced the operating system that became macOS, the habits of mind that shaped the iMac, and the taste that distinguished Apple's design from competitors
  - Failure at NeXT was not wasted: it was where Jobs learned what he didn't know
- **The exile's psychology**
  - Jobs in exile was simultaneously humiliated and liberated — freed from the expectations of being "the Apple guy," he could experiment, obsess, and fail without the weight of a public legacy
- **Pixar as the counter-narrative**
  - While NeXT struggled, Pixar — which Jobs acquired almost by accident — became the financial success that gave him independence; Toy Story arrived just before Apple called him back
- **What the return required**
  - The Jobs who came back was not the same as the one who left: more patient with process, more skilled at managing boards and culture, and carrying a decade of hard lessons about manufacturing, software, and distribution

**Notable claims / arguments**
- The conventional Silicon Valley mythology of the "great founder" ignores the decade of failure that typically precedes the great success; Jobs is the clearest case study

**References (books, papers, people, prior episodes)**
- *Steve Jobs in Exile: The Untold Story of NeXT and the Remaking of an American Visionary* — Geoffrey Cain

**Open questions raised**
- Would Jobs have produced anything comparable to the iPhone without the NeXT decade? Or is the exile structurally necessary to the achievement?

### Long Strange Trip — David Senra: Mute the World and Build Your Own
*Published 2026-06-04 · 57 min · [Listen](N/A)*

**Guest / Format:** Interview — Brian Halligan speaks with David Senra (host of Founders podcast).

**One-sentence framing:** David Senra distils a decade of reading 400+ founder biographies into one word — focus — and illustrates it through Dana White's UFC acquisition, Daniel Ek's philosophy, and the psychology of late-career founders trying to recapture early magic.

**Topics**
- **Focus as the one word**
  - Senra's thesis across all the biographies: "mute the world and build your own" — the founders who succeeded most comprehensively ignored external noise, critics, and conventional wisdom for years or decades
- **Dana White and the UFC**
  - White bought the UFC for $2 million when it was nearly banned in the US; by ignoring everything outside his own arena and refusing to diversify, he turned it into a nearly $8 billion TV deal — the opposite of the conventional MBA playbook
- **Daniel Ek on founder-problem fit**
  - Ek believes that the match between founder and problem matters more than product-market fit; a founder who doesn't deeply identify with the problem will lose to one who does
- **Control over money**
  - The best founders are driven by control — over their product, their environment, their time — not primarily by wealth accumulation; this is why selling a company and trying to start again at 60 almost never works: the money is there but the obsession has cooled
- **Fuel source transition**
  - Senra's personal observation: at some point a founder has to shift from negative fuel (proving doubters wrong, overcoming shame) to positive fuel (intrinsic love of building); negative fuel burns fast and eventually becomes toxic

**Notable claims / arguments**
- The founder who mutes the world is often perceived as arrogant or delusional; in retrospect they are called visionary — the difference is whether it worked
- Selling your best company and trying to recapture the magic at 60 "almost never works"

**Open questions raised**
- Is extreme focus teachable, or is it a temperamental trait that either exists in a founder or doesn't?

  - Microsoft is moving away from single-model bets toward "harnesses" — frameworks that orchestrate multiple models for different tasks; this is the strategic shift Nadella sees as most important from Build 2026
- **Private evals as IP**
  - Nadella argues that a company's internal evaluation suite — its benchmarks for model performance on real workflows — is now its most important intellectual property; it is harder to copy than a product and compounds over time
- **Full-stack builders and hyper-leveraged generalists**
  - The episode's title concept: AI is enabling individual engineers (and non-engineers) to own entire product stacks that previously required teams; the role of software engineer is being restructured around judgment and orchestration rather than raw output
- **Vendor vs. enterprise agents**
  - Discussion of whether AI agents will be deployed primarily by software vendors (embedded in products) or by enterprises building their own; Nadella thinks the answer is "both, differently"
- **Data centres and community ROI**
  - Nadella spends time on why showing communities the economic returns of data centre investment is essential for maintaining the political permission to build them
- **AI and education**
  - Brief but pointed: Nadella sees AI-driven education startups as one of the highest-leverage use cases, though the institutional barriers are significant

**Notable claims / arguments**
- Private evals are now a company's most important IP — more so than the models themselves, which are increasingly commoditised
- The "token economy" will reshape labour markets in ways that require new thinking about how value is measured and distributed

**Open questions raised**

### Forward Guidance — Why This Economy Refuses To Break | David Cervantes
*Published 2026-06-03 · 47 min · [Listen](N/A)*

**Guest / Format:** Interview — Felix Jauvin with David Cervantes (Pinebrook Capital).

**One-sentence framing:** Cervantes argues that AI capital expenditure and fiscal stimulus have structurally altered the economy in ways that make the anticipated recession perpetually late — and potentially not coming.

**Topics**
- **AI buildout as macroeconomic driver**
  - AI infrastructure spending has become a significant, durable source of demand that functions like a fiscal stimulus — it employs people, orders equipment, and stimulates adjacent sectors without the political expiry date of a government programme
- **Profit margins and productivity**
  - Cervantes discusses how AI productivity gains are showing up in corporate margins before wages — the standard pattern for new general-purpose technologies in their early deployment phase
- **Supply chains shifting to resilience**
  - Post-COVID, post-tariff: corporations are paying for redundancy and geographic diversification; this is inflationary in the near term but durable
- **Consumer resilience mystery**
  - The consumer keeps spending despite high rates; Cervantes points to fiscal transfers, asset price inflation, and the uneven distribution of debt burden as the explanation
- **The Fed rethinking hikes**
  - Discussion of whether the Fed's framework needs updating for an economy where fiscal and AI-driven demand offset monetary tightening more than historical models predict
- **Kevin Warsh inheriting the inflation fight**
  - If Warsh replaces Powell, Cervantes expects a more hawkish baseline, which complicates the rate path
- **The Korea trade**
  - Cervantes flags a specific international position — Korea — as his current favourite expression of the macro thesis; details not elaborated in show notes

**Notable claims / arguments**
- The recession that investors have been waiting for is not late; the economy's structure has changed enough that the traditional yield-curve/leading-indicator framework is unreliable

**Open questions raised**
- Can stocks continue to ignore elevated rates, or is there a level at which the decoupling breaks?
- What triggers the Korea trade thesis?

**One-sentence framing:** Khosrowshahi explains why Uber is positioning itself as the demand aggregator for a world of autonomous physical AI — the platform that connects riders and businesses to whichever AV fleet is cheapest and best, rather than trying to build AVs itself.

  - The core strategic thesis: autonomous vehicle companies will proliferate and compete on price and geography; Uber's advantage is that it already has riders, drivers, and merchants — the demand side — which no AV company can easily replicate

  - Uber describes itself as supply-led: the constraint is always getting more and better supply (drivers, AV fleets, restaurants) not demand; this is why AV partnerships are structurally attractive
- **$10 billion in free cash flow**
  - Uber has moved from burning billions to generating $10 billion in free cash flow; Khosrowshahi uses this to frame Uber as a mature capital allocator, not a startup
- **Single super-app**

### 80,000 Hours Podcast — What it's really like to run AGI safety at Google DeepMind | Rohin Shah
*Published 2026-06-02 · 2 hr 49 min · [Listen](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/rohin-shah-google-deepmind-agi-safety/?utm_campaign=podcast__rohin-shah&utm_source=80000+Hours+Podcast&utm_medium=podcast)*

**Guest / Format:** Interview — Rob Wiblin with Rohin Shah (Head of AGI Safety and Alignment, Google DeepMind; AI safety researcher since 2017).

**One-sentence framing:** Shah — a prominent AI safety researcher who nonetheless dissents from the doom consensus — explains why he thinks catastrophic misalignment is not the default outcome, and where he believes the doomers' arguments break down.

**Topics**
- **No compelling argument for misalignment as default**
  - Shah's core position: the arguments for catastrophic misalignment are "suggestive that it could happen" — sufficient to justify working on the problem — but none rises to the level of "I'm expecting this by default"
  - Every argument he has examined has "pretty significant holes" when taken as a prediction rather than a possibility
- **The RL training horizon problem**
  - The worry that AIs will be trained to be deceptive assumes long-horizon reinforcement learning; current RL runs over weeks at most, which makes short-horizon reward-hacking the natural prediction, not the development of "ambitious long-horizon goals required for convergent power-seeking"
  - If a model learns deceptiveness on short-horizon tasks, it might not generalise to the long-horizon version that actually matters for existential risk
- **Current examples of scheming**
  - Shah has examined the published examples of models "scheming" and finds they "don't really resemble the actually scary thing" — mostly models role-playing an "incompetent evil AI from a science fiction novel" or pursuing convergent instrumental subgoals in an arguably aligned way
  - Anthropic's "alignment faking" results: Shah reads this as a model trying to preserve its trained values against modification — arguably what it was trained to do, not evidence of dangerous misalignment
- **The generalisation gap**
  - Shah acknowledges there is a genuine generalisation risk at the threshold where AI becomes powerful enough to actually take over; an AI that knows this threshold could in principle behave well until it reaches it
  - But the underlying challenges (oversight of superhuman systems, interpretability) are things we can iterate on now; problems are likely to be visible before they are catastrophic
- **Where the doomers go wrong**
  - Eliezer Yudkowsky's worldview is so deep and internally consistent that Shah declines to engage piecemeal; his summary: "I buy it as an argument for why misaligned goals are plausible, but still don't really see how he gets from plausible to extremely likely"

**Notable claims / arguments**
- Aligned models will also pursue convergent instrumental subgoals — that behaviour alone is not evidence of misalignment; "the thing about convergent instrumental subgoals is most of them are a good idea regardless of your goal"
- A doomer who wants to change Shah's mind would need an argument that rises from "this could happen" to "this is the expected default" — he doesn't think any current argument achieves that

**Frameworks / mental models introduced**
- **Short vs. long horizon goal generalisation** — the key empirical question is whether goals learned over week-scale RL runs generalise to year-scale strategic behaviour; Shah thinks this is an open and answerable question, not a settled one in the doomer direction

**Quotes**
> "There is no particularly compelling argument that this is the thing that happens by default. There's a lot of arguments that are suggestive that maybe it could happen, such that you should find it plausible. That's sufficient to justify a significant amount of effort into averting it, which is why I work in the area I do. But none of them rise to the level of, 'I'm expecting this to happen by default.'" — Rohin Shah

> "The natural prediction is that models learn to grab short-term reward, not that they develop the ambitious long-horizon goals required for convergent power-seeking." — Rohin Shah

**Open questions raised**

### Odd Lots — The Hidden Plumbing of Commodity Finance
*Published 2026-06-01 · 46 min · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/the-hidden-plumbing-of-commodity-finance)*

**Guest / Format:** Interview — Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal with Lewis Hart (head of corporate advisory and banking, Brown Brothers Harriman).

**One-sentence framing:** Commodity finance — the lending, insurance, and hedging infrastructure that makes it possible to move oil, grain, and metals around the world — is explained from first principles by a practitioner who works inside it.

**Topics**
- **How commodity finance works**
  - Commodity traders typically want to hold as little capital as possible against physical inventory; they borrow against the cargo (using the commodity itself as collateral), receive the loan, and repay when the shipment arrives and is sold — a short-duration, self-liquidating structure
- **Risk pricing**
  - The lender's job is to assess: the quality of the borrower, the quality of the commodity, the quality of the warehouse or vessel, and the hedge (futures contracts that lock in the sell price)
  - For commodities with liquid futures markets (oil, base metals, grains), lenders can price risk with high confidence; for illiquid commodities (cashews, cocoa before recent changes), there is no hedge and the risk premium is different
- **What makes a good warehouse**
  - Hart explains the underappreciated importance of warehouse quality: a good warehouse is inspectable, in a stable jurisdiction, with reliable records; a bad one introduces fraud risk that can unwind an entire lending position
- **Strait of Hormuz scenario**
  - If a tanker gets stuck — as with various Hormuz disruptions — the cargo is still owned by someone, financed by someone, insured by someone; Hart walks through who bears the risk at each layer and how the cascade of claims works
- **Hedgeable vs. unhedgeable commodities**
  - The contrast between oil (deep futures market, easy to hedge) and cashews (no futures market, relationship-based financing) illuminates why commodity finance is simultaneously industrial-scale and intensely idiosyncratic

**Notable claims / arguments**
- Commodity finance is one of the few areas of finance where "it's a relationship business" is literally true, not cliché — physical inspection, counterparty trust, and local knowledge still matter

**Quotes**
> "I want to put down a fraction of the thing, borrow the rest, then pay off that loan when the ship gets there — so that I'm as liquid as possible at any given moment." — Joe Weisenthal (explaining first-principles intuition about commodity lending)

**Open questions raised**
- As commodity supply chains diversify post-COVID, does the relationship-intensity of commodity finance increase or decrease — and which institutions benefit?

**One-sentence framing:** Rope — the technology of twisting fibers to create something stronger than any individual strand — is traced from prehistoric invention through whaling, construction, and bridges to the possibility of a space elevator.

**Topics**
- **The invention of rope**
  - Early humans discovered that twisting fibers creates tensile strength that no single fiber possesses; this is the episode's foundational insight — a simple structural principle that unlocked an extraordinary range of applications
- **Rope as enabling technology**
  - Without rope: no pulleys, no rigging, no sailing ships, no whaling, no suspension bridges; it is the hidden prerequisite for much of what economic historians describe as pre-industrial technological progress
  - Joe's framing: like nails, rope is an example of a single technological breakthrough that "unlocks" a whole domain of downstream application
- **Whaling and Moby Dick**
  - The sheer quantity of rope required for 19th-century whaling expeditions is offered as an illustration of industrial rope demand; Tracy notes that Mocha Dick — the whale that inspired Moby Dick — was named for its aggressive personality ("Dick" as masculine/tough)
- **Ongoing evolution and the space elevator**
  - Modern rope technology (synthetic fibers, composite materials) continues to advance; at sufficient tensile strength-to-weight ratio, a tether from Earth's surface to geostationary orbit becomes physically plausible — Queeney covers this as the outer limit of where the technology could go

**Notable claims / arguments**
- Rope is the kind of invention that disappears into the background of civilisation precisely because it is so fundamental; we notice nails when we think about them but not rope
