# Podcast digest — 2026-07-17

20 new episodes across 14 podcasts since the last digest.

The thread running through this window is disempowerment by increment — the discovery, across several unrelated fields, that losing ground rarely arrives as one dramatic event. A Carnegie fellow prices out what it would cost a coalition of middle powers to build their own frontier AI model (something like $500 billion today, rising every month they wait) and concludes they'll probably just rent access from Washington instead. New York's governor pauses data-center construction not because she's turned against AI but because the grid math doesn't pencil out yet. A big-law chair watches the price of routine legal work fall 70% and somehow bills more hours than ever, because cheaper output just means clients order more of it. And a Supreme Court ruling that supposedly protects the Federal Reserve's independence turns out to rest on a legal theory only two of nine justices actually believe. Underneath all of it, the same instinct shows up in a different key: an hour spent rebuilding Einstein's field equations from a single "beautiful coincidence," and a DeepMind team trying to build the equivalent of an MRI for a neural network's mind — the same faith that careful reasoning, applied long enough, can make the invisible legible. Whether that faith holds up against oil chokepoints, a World Cup final, and nine centuries of magical tradition is left, appropriately, an open question.

### 80,000 Hours Podcast — "How middle powers avoid losing everything in a post-AI world | Anton Leicht"

**Guest:** Anton Leicht (Fellow, Carnegie Endowment for International Peace; writer, "Threading the Needle") · **Hosts:** Tom Reed · **Aired:** 2026-07-14 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/anton-leicht-middle-powers-agi-left-behind/?utm_campaign=podcast__anton-leicht&utm_source=80000+Hours+Podcast&utm_medium=podcast)

**Thesis:** Anton Leicht argues that once frontier AI becomes a core economic input, countries without their own frontier models will be reduced to customers — capturing all the risk and little of the upside — and face a slow, compounding relative decline even if they remain "fine" in absolute terms. He has costed a coalition-built frontier model (EU, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Korea, Japan, ideally Taiwan) at roughly $500 billion over four years and around 3 million Blackwell-class chips, but doubts it happens, since no treasury will fund a speculative venture with no business case that invites US retaliation — and the price only rises the longer everyone waits.

- **The $500 billion moonshot, and why it probably dies in committee.** Leicht calls the coalition-funded frontier model "not an absurd idea on paper," but says delay is its own tax: wait six months and it's $600 billion; wait longer and it becomes "like $1 trillion."
- **Compute-for-access as the realistic fallback.** Rather than build a rival model, Leicht advocates trading domestic data-centre buildout (an 18–24 month window for a gigawatt) for guaranteed frontier-model access "at least at parity with the US commercial market" — citing a scaled-back Stargate Norway and a collapsed UK-US Technology Prosperity Deal as cautionary tales.
- **The "Fable incident" as inflection point.** Leicht says the episode — where export-controlling model weights or outputs suddenly became "a much more realistic prospect" in DC policy circles — accelerated a shift he wasn't expecting this soon, though he calls the specific China-tie allegations "a red herring" for access-parity purposes.
- **Upstream and downstream bottlenecks as leverage.** His structural fallback: own the inputs (ASML's lithography machines, chipmaking, exclusive data) and the outputs (production plants, clinical trials) that "a country of geniuses in a data centre" still can't replace — something he says Europeans, Japanese, and South Koreans are already good at.
- **Screen foreign buyouts now, before the price triples.** Asked about Jeff Bezos's new venture buying up German manufacturing, Leicht says "definitely screen for the FDI" — outsiders are exploiting an "awareness gap," buying industrial assets for a few billion that will be worth vastly more once integrated into AI supply chains.
- **Flexible labor markets over job lock-in.** Counterintuitively, Leicht argues middle powers should loosen labor markets, not freeze them, so workers can move into whatever "jagged frontier" AI can't do — warning that rigid markets risk jumping straight from a manageable slowdown to "15% unemployment" with no path to reallocate.

### Odd Lots — "NY Governor Kathy Hochul on Her One Year Data Center Moratorium"

**Guest:** Kathy Hochul (Governor of New York) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-07-15 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/ny-governor-kathy-hochul-on-her-one-year-data-center-moratorium)

**Thesis:** On July 14, 2026, New York became the first US state to impose a formal one-year moratorium on new large data-center construction. Hochul frames it not as anti-AI protectionism but as a deliberate pause — her Public Service Commission has a year to force developers to bring their own power generation or pay a premium for grid access — while she simultaneously touts a $100 billion Micron semiconductor investment, a push to reverse the Indian Point nuclear shutdown, and a paused Waymo rollout meant to buy time for New York's professional drivers.

### Odd Lots — "Why AI Might Actually Create More Work for Lawyers"

**Guest:** Gary Wingens (Chair and partner, Lowenstein Sandler; structured-finance lawyer by background) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-07-13 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/why-ai-might-actually-create-more-work-for-lawyers)

**Thesis:** Gary Wingens argues AI is cutting the cost of routine legal work — he cites a due-diligence project reviewing thousands of trust agreements whose price fell roughly 70% over a few years — but rather than shrinking the legal industry, it's triggering a Jevons paradox: as each unit of legal work gets cheaper, clients order more of it. One Fortune 50 client's internal patent requests have "quadrupled" as AI lowers the cost of drafting, and American Lawyer data shows average hourly rates at top firms rose 10.1% in 2025 against roughly 3% CPI growth — the billable hour becoming more valuable, not less, even as total spend per matter falls.

- **The $10 million project that became $3 million — and got approved.** Using illustrative numbers, Wingens describes a trust-review project a client declined at a $10 million quote; once AI cut the cost roughly 70%, the client said yes at $3 million, turning zero revenue into new revenue that wouldn't otherwise have existed.
- **Blacklining as the historical precedent.** Wingens recalls hand-marking documents as a young associate; once blacklining software arrived, clients stopped paying for a task that now takes about ninety seconds — yet, he says, "somehow, when the machines took that job away from me, I still had plenty of work to do... my life got more interesting."
- **AI-to-AI negotiation, already happening.** In patent prosecution, sophisticated tech clients now run the firm's draft patents through their own AI tools and return AI-generated comments — "we're almost at the point where their AI agent is talking to our AI agent."
- **Patent requests have quadrupled.** A client told Wingens that because their own engineers use AI, the volume of internal invention disclosures and patent requests has "quadrupled," even as per-patent drafting costs fall — his clearest evidence for the Jevons-paradox thesis.
- **Rates up 10.1% while total legal spend falls.** Citing American Lawyer's 2025 data, Wingens notes hourly rates at the largest US firms rose 10.1% (more at the most profitable top 20) against roughly 3% inflation — his read is that AI has made the billable hour itself more productive.
- **Training juniors without the grunt work.** With blacklining-style tasks gone, the firm now has first-years bring laptops to training and query AI tools directly for draft contract provisions, building a shared internal database of deal terms in place of hands-on drafting practice.

> "When I was a first year associate, I was hand blacklining documents and clients were paying me by the hour to hand blackline documents. Within two years, we had blacklining software and they no longer paid me by the hour, and it took roughly ninety seconds to blackline a document." — Gary Wingens

> "Ten million dollars, they said, no, we're not going to do it, and our revenue was zero. When it came down to three million, they said, that's worth it, and we had three million of revenue." — Gary Wingens

## PHYSICS, MACHINES & KNOWING THE UNKNOWABLE

### Dwarkesh Podcast — "Adam Brown – A deep but accessible introduction to general relativity"

**Guest:** Adam Brown (leads Blueshift, the science-and-reasoning team at Google DeepMind; formerly taught general relativity at Stanford) · **Hosts:** Dwarkesh Patel · **Aired:** 2026-07-10 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/adam-brown-gr)

**Thesis:** Adam Brown rebuilds, from scratch, how Einstein got from a single "coincidence" — that inertial mass and gravitational mass are identical to one part in 10^15 — to the conclusion that gravity isn't a force but the curvature of spacetime, formalized in his 1915 field equations. He then uses that framework to explain black holes as, in principle, 100%-efficient power plants (versus roughly 10⁻¹⁰ efficiency for chemical rockets), why infalling and distant observers experience crossing an event horizon in completely incompatible ways, and closes on whether AI reasoning alone — without Einstein's decades of false starts — could have derived general relativity from first principles.

- **The coincidence Einstein called his most beautiful thought.** Inertial mass (resistance to force) and gravitational mass (the "charge" in Newton's gravity law) had no logical reason to be equal — unlike electrostatics, where mass and charge are unrelated — yet they matched to 1 part in 1,000 in Newton's time and to 1 part in 10^15 today.
- **The airplane-map analogy for curved spacetime.** A flat map makes a great-circle flight path over Greenland look like a curved detour because the map is "trying to pretend that the Earth is flat"; similarly, a falling piece of chalk is moving in a straight line through curved spacetime, while a person standing still is the one deviating from a straight line.
- **Einstein's equation in one slogan.** "Matter tells spacetime how to curve," and in turn "the curvature of spacetime tells matter how to move" — producing what look like "fictitious forces" to anyone who assumes spacetime is flat.
- **Black holes as the ultimate power plant.** Lowering a brick from Earth's surface extracts only about 7×10⁻¹⁰ of its rest-mass energy under Newtonian gravity; lowering it to just above a black hole's event horizon extracts essentially 100% of its mc² — because "gravity can touch" the rest-mass energy that chemistry and nuclear fission or fusion (at roughly 10⁻¹⁰ and 10⁻² efficiency, respectively) cannot.
- **A coincidence Newton's own math almost predicted.** 18th-century scientists Michell and Laplace had already derived the critical radius at which escape velocity equals light speed using purely Newtonian reasoning — and it happens to exactly match the true general-relativistic Schwarzschild radius, "including crazily this factor of 2," for entirely unrelated reasons.
- **Doomed, but not dead.** From outside, a person falling into a black hole appears to slow, redshift, and fade to black without ever visibly crossing the horizon; from their own perspective, crossing feels ordinary, and for a sufficiently large black hole tidal forces at the horizon are negligible enough that "you could live out your entire life" before reaching the singularity.
- **Three separate proofs black holes are real.** Decades of stars orbiting the invisible, compact Sagittarius A* at our galaxy's center; LIGO's 2015 detection of gravitational waves from two merging ~30-solar-mass black holes 1.6 billion light-years away; and the Event Horizon Telescope's direct image of infalling matter.

> "So Einstein leapt: could it be the case, and this was his central idea, that gravity itself is an inertial force?" — Adam Brown

> "When you cross the event horizon, you are doomed... you must proceed to the singularity... You are doomed, but you are not dead." — Adam Brown

> "I never see you cross the event horizon. I just see you getting closer and closer... you just fade to black, fade through red to black." — Adam Brown, describing how a distant observer watches someone fall into a black hole

## THE FED UNDER SIEGE & THE MACRO WEEK

### Odd Lots — "Lev Menand and Nathan Tankus on Why Fed Independence Is Now Hanging by a Thread"

**Guests:** Lev Menand (Professor, Columbia Law School), Nathan Tankus (writer and president, Notes on the Crises) · **Hosts:** Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal · **Aired:** 2026-07-17 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/lev-menand-and-nathan-tankus-on-why-fed-independence-is-now)

**Thesis:** In June 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 5-4 in *Trump v. Cook* that Fed governor Lisa Cook could not be summarily fired, carving out a Fed exception even as a companion ruling (*Slaughter*) stripped removal protections from officials at agencies like the FTC, gutting the 1935 precedent *Humphrey's Executor*. Menand and Tankus argue the Fed carve-out is legally incoherent — the Court borrowed its "history and tradition" test from a Second Amendment case and anchored Fed independence in Alexander Hamilton's First Bank of the United States, even though that bank was a private, investor-owned institution with no executive officers at all.

- **A 5-4 ruling almost nobody actually believes in.** Menand notes only two of nine justices — Roberts and Kavanaugh — think the resulting Fed carve-out makes doctrinal sense; the three liberal justices dissented because they think the FTC deserved protection too, while conservatives like Thomas think the Fed shouldn't have it either.
- **The gun-rights case that became the template.** The Court imported its "history and tradition" method from *United States v. Rahimi*, a Second Amendment case, to test whether Fed independence has a comparable historical analog — an approach Menand calls unprecedented for agency-removal law.
- **Hamilton's bank wasn't a government body.** The First Bank of the United States was investor-owned, 20% government-subscribed and later fully private, with no executive officers — making it, in Menand's telling, "not in any way abrogating the president's rights to remove executive officers," and a poor analog for insulating the modern Fed's regulators.
- **The Fed funds itself, and always has.** Tankus details how the Fed Board funds itself by assessing the technically-private, bank-owned regional Federal Reserve Banks (like the New York Fed, owned by banks such as JPMorgan Chase) — assessments the Board can raise at will, insulating it from both Congress and the White House.
- **From weakest protections to strongest, "just by mowing down everyone else."** Tankus notes the 1913 Federal Reserve Act never defined "cause" for removal, historically leaving the Fed with the weakest removal protections of any independent agency — now, after the Court gutted protections everywhere else, the Fed has the strongest, almost by accident.
- **Courts may be weighing legitimacy against market panic.** Menand suggests the justices may prefer "an incoherent legal explanation for the carve-out" to the alternative of Trump firing the entire Fed board and risking a market "tailspin" — a dynamic he traces to 19th-century Justice Joseph Story's tolerance of state-bank practices to avoid destabilizing the antebellum monetary system.

> "Now the Fed is not safe. This is not a good situation that the Fed has found itself in. The Fed is an endangered species." — Lev Menand

> "Necessity becomes substance of law, no matter how much people think they're formalist, even two centuries ago." — Nathan Tankus

### Odd Lots — "Why Soccer Analytics Works Like Volatility Arbitrage Trading"

**Guests:** Mike Treacy (Head of Risk, Apex Fintech Solutions; former volatility arbitrage trader; advises MLS club Austin FC), Joris Bekkers (soccer analytics consultant) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway · **Aired:** 2026-07-16 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/why-soccer-analytics-works-like-volatility-arbitrage-trading)

**Thesis:** Soccer was long dismissed as too fluid for the "moneyball" data revolution, but the sport has undergone its own quantitative turn since Chris Anderson and David Sally's 2013 book *The Numbers Game* and the Twitter-era spread of Expected Goals (xG) charts. Treacy argues soccer analytics resembles volatility-arbitrage trading because every facet of the game is a distribution of highly levered bets on imperfect information — and the field has progressed from on-ball event data to positional tracking to full body-pose data, illustrated by the 2026 FIFA World Cup generating an estimated 90-plus petabytes across 104 matches, a 45-fold jump from 2022.

- **A 45-fold data explosion.** The 2026 World Cup's 104 matches will generate more than 90 petabytes of data — a 45-fold increase over 2022 — driven mostly by dense body-pose tracking rather than simple on-ball events.
- **"Every aspect of the game is a distribution."** Treacy says team and player performance, compounded by promotion/relegation, make soccer overlap with volatility trading because both involve "highly levered bets on often imperfect information," unlike more predictive sports like baseball.
- **xG traces to one book and a Twitter habit.** Treacy credits *The Numbers Game* for reframing soccer as a "weakest link" game, and analyst Michael Caley for popularizing weekly Expected Goals charts on Twitter, sparking debate over what xG actually measures.
- **Models "censor" abnormal games.** Treacy recalls a match where Tottenham, down to nine men, pushed a high line and let Chelsea's Nicholas Jackson score three goals in one game — roughly 20% of his season total — a game state so abnormal his models remove it entirely rather than let it skew per-90-minute stats.

- **Data sourcing can be improvised.** Building models for Bournemouth in 2016, Treacy had no idea where to find usable data, so he posted a job on Upwork and found a Ukraine-based developer with connections to European football bettors — an operation he calls "very skunk works."
