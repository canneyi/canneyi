### Odd Lots — "An Election Day Special With Nate Silver"

**Guest:** Nate Silver (founder, Silver Bulletin; built 538) · **Hosts:** Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway (recorded October 29) · **Aired:** 2026-11-03 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim (transcript shows auto-transcription artifacts on proper nouns).* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/an-election-day-special-with-nate-silver)

**Thesis:** Silver calls the midterms the "rock versus the hard place election." Conditions favour Democrats: Trump's approval is about 37%, which by some measures is the worst for a president at a midterm since Truman in 1946, and Democrats lead the generic ballot by about nine points. But to take the Senate they must win states as red as Texas, Kansas and Alaska. His model puts Democrats in the high 70s for the Senate, against about 68% on Polymarket and Kalshi.

- **Trump is the central character.** Republicans have dropped him from their ads, but he keeps putting himself in the story. Silver doubts James Tallarico would lead in Texas if Trump hadn't endorsed Ken Paxton. Without the Iran war he thinks the Senate would be a toss-up. Only 19 points of Trump's 37% approval is strong approval.
- **The "polls always miss Democrats high" story is wrong.** Polls underestimated Trump in 2016, 2020 and 2024. In Trump-era races without him on the ballot, that error hasn't shown up. Last year they badly underestimated Democrats in the New Jersey and Virginia governor races. His explanation: Trump draws low-turnout voters who are hard to poll, and they don't turn out for a Roger Marshall or a Ken Paxton.
- **Pollsters are hedging.** Marist's Maine poll had Democrat Troy Jackson up nine among registered voters. Its likely-voter model cut that to two, and the governor's race went from 11 to zero. Silver says the math "literally doesn't add up." He reads it as pollsters fearing a miss high on Democrats.
- **AI won't replace pollsters.** Synthetic respondents come out as stereotypes: "99%" of synthetic Hispanic respondents love soccer. Turnout is the hard part. In his South Carolina test, Black turnout at 2024 rates and white turnout at 2022 rates moved the topline by about 10 points.
- **Prediction markets track polls plus "vibes."** His model is 0.995 correlated with Polymarket state by state. When it launched on August 11 it moved markets by several points. He thinks markets lean Republican, and would be "extremely terrible" if they couldn't see polls.
- **Tests for the Democratic Party.** Michigan, where Abdul El-Sayed beat Haley Stevens in the primary, is the test for progressives. Kansas, where pastor Adam Hamilton is up four in a CNN poll, is the test for moderates. AI is the one policy area where he expects action before 2028.
- **AI as coding help.** NFL playoff tiebreakers took him two and a half days last year. Now a model writes them in 15 minutes, and the code runs 10 times faster. He still has to guide it on about two-thirds of prompts.

### Odd Lots — "The $300 Billion Structured Notes Market Is Booming"

**Guest:** Alex Micalizzi (Nomura; managing director and international head of equity structured notes) · **Hosts:** Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal · **Aired:** 2026-11-02 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim (transcript shows auto-transcription artifacts on proper nouns).* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/the-300-billion-structured-notes-market-is-booming)

**Thesis:** A structured note is a bank IOU plus a derivative. The buyer gives up interest, takes the issuer's credit risk, and usually sells the bank a put option in return for a coupon. Micalizzi says SEC-registered US notes are on track for about $300 billion this year, up from about $200 billion last year. Investors now use these notes to bet on the AI trade. Dealer hedging can move markets.

- **How an autocallable works.** If the index is above its starting level on a check date, the note "knocks out": the buyer gets $100 back plus a coupon. The bank pays more because the put it holds loses value as markets rise. A typical buffer is a 60% knock-in, so an S&P drop of 20% is still protected until it hits down 40%. Below that, the buyer takes the full loss.
- **Worst-ofs drive the growth.** S&P notes are the biggest single category. Worst-of autocallables on the S&P, NASDAQ and Russell have grown fastest. The payout follows the weakest index, so the coupon is higher. The Russell went sideways for years and has risen in "a straight line" since Liberation Day. That triggered repeated knockouts, and reissuance pushed volumes up.
- **Who buys.** Mostly high-net-worth clients through private banks. The product is older in Asia: it started in Japan when rates hit zero (Uridashi). One CoreWeave note in Asia paid a 40% annualised yield.
- **Dealers end up with vega to sell.** Banks hedge delta first, then sell volatility in the listed market. When markets fall, notes that would have knocked out after three months stay alive. Dealers then have more vega to sell just as market makers pull back and some buyers of options disappear. A fast rally can leave dealers short their hedges. US notes typically run three to five years with an expected life of about two years, so a selloff can extend that exposure to five years.
- **Single stocks are binary.** Single-stock worst-of-three notes often run 6 to 18 months, with daily checks after the first month. That creates an "on off" market in near-dated options on semis and hyperscalers. Close to barriers, dealers can be short gamma, which exaggerates moves.
- **A slow grind is the worst case.** The ideal for a buyer is high volatility at entry, then a quiet drift lower, so the coupon keeps paying. In 2018, a drift down hurt dealers, and the industry built a new product in response.

> "And generally, what provokes or what creates these sort of structures is what created pain." — Alex Micalizzi

### 80,000 Hours Podcast — "An AI disaster hits. The president calls Xi. Then what? | Jeremie Harris (crosspost from NonZero)"

**Guest:** Jeremie Harris (CEO and cofounder, Gladstone AI; cohost of Last Week in AI; coauthor of a 2024 State Department–commissioned action plan on AI risk) · **Hosts:** Robert Wright (NonZero), with an intro by Rob Wiblin · **Aired:** 2026-11-05 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://www.nonzero.org/p/unlocked-debating-the-ai-china-question)

**Thesis:** Harris says arguments won't slow AI down. Events will. He expects an "AI Chernobyl": a disaster bad enough to flip the US government's official view of AGI risk. He defines it by that response, not by a body count. If a thousand deaths isn't enough, "raise your waterline 10x more." Then the president calls Xi. China is six months behind, so a domestic pause means nothing without it. There will be no verification tech ready, so the first deal will be crude, costly and backed by threats.

- **The first deal will run on satellites and threats.** With only national technical means, red lines will sound like a cluster "above 500 megawatts" or heat signatures consistent with a big training run. The US would keep its response ambiguous: maybe cutting an undersea cable, maybe a cyberattack. Harris expects the US to start with a unilateral slowdown. He says verification roadmaps run "six months, 12 months." But most verification startups have never spoken to the US intelligence community. He calls David Sacks's dismissal of verification "almost a criminal lack of agency."
- **Capabilities scale. Safety doesn't.** Capabilities ride one automatable number, compute efficiency. Safety depends on a handful of people. He says there has been "approximately zero progress" on superalignment. His median estimate once had us "dead by now."
- **OpenAI is losing its safety people.** Harris describes "evaporative cooling": round after round, the most safety-minded staff leave. Altman promised 20% of compute to superalignment and didn't deliver. Staff saw the Hugging Face incident coming and were ignored. Altman is said to be bullish on a 2027 roadmap aimed at an automated AI researcher. Harris thinks Anthropic may be two training runs ahead of what is public.
- **Open Phil steered people away from Washington.** Harris says Open Philanthropy (now Coefficient Giving) told outsiders not to say "AGI" to officials. It feared they would launch a Manhattan Project. Gladstone has taken no grants from effective altruist sources. Still, he says the funder got it "much more right than almost anyone."
- **Alignment progress could break a deal.** If China comes to believe an alignment breakthrough has cut loss-of-control risk "from 80% down to 20%," it may suspect the US will defect. So enforcement has to back up verification.
- **China has deep access to US labs.** Harris says "dozens" of OpenAI employees come from Tsinghua or BAAI. On some teams, the share with access to algorithmic secrets is "sometimes north of 50%." He admits his story about Chinese nationals checking in with handlers is unsourced: "Calibrate accordingly." He warns that US cyber superiority, its main equaliser, could disappear if AI ends up favouring defence.
- **Wright pushes back.** He argues the mistrust runs both ways, citing Libya and the Iran deal. He prefers symmetric "organic transparency" to threatening a nuclear power. Harris wants a bloc of allies for leverage, not for ideology: South Korea for Samsung and SK Hynix, Taiwan for TSMC.

> "No one knows how to make a curve of control or a curve of alignment." — Jeremie Harris

> "This is a profound source of destabilisation that no one has grappled with for a long time." — Jeremie Harris

> "China is in our bones in a way that we are not in their bones." — Jeremie Harris

### 80,000 Hours Podcast — "3 new scandals show being a pain in the ass for AI labs is a legit job now | Rob Wiblin"

**Guest:** None (solo episode, recorded October 29, 2026) · **Host:** Rob Wiblin · **Aired:** 2026-11-03 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/hold-frontier-ai-companies-accountable/?utm_campaign=podcast__rob-wiblin&utm_source=80000+Hours+Podcast&utm_medium=podcast)

Wiblin argues that pressure from insiders, journalists and campaigners has worked well for how little effort has gone into it. - **Anthropic's brake is hard to pull.** Under the draft bill, only the Commerce Secretary could seek a halt on training, and only before a single federal court in DC. The government would have to prove the risk was imminent. Stop orders would lapse after 45 days. - **The OpenAI firings look like a leak hunt gone wrong.** Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni and Jasmine Wang were fired in simultaneous 2pm meetings. The Wall Street Journal links this to a September 1 story in The Information. It reported that OpenAI's Astra model uses "looped transformers," which make chain of thought harder to monitor. Almost 400 OpenAI staff had signed a petition this year to slow AI down, so many people could have objected.
- **DeepMind's gag clause lasts for life.** Staff told the BBC in mid-October that their UK contracts bar them from ever publicly criticising Google. It came to light when ex-staff wanted to criticise AI sales to the US Department of War. OpenAI and Anthropic dropped similar terms two years ago.

- **Insiders should speak up now.** Their leverage may vanish once their work is automated. He advises arguing the merits in public Slack channels and resigning publicly only if that fails.

## POWER, HISTORY AND IDEAS

### Dwarkesh Podcast — "What Robert Caro learned about power after studying it for 50 years"

**Guest:** Robert Caro (biographer; author of *The Power Broker* on Robert Moses; has spent 50 years writing about Lyndon Johnson) · **Host:** Dwarkesh Patel · **Aired:** 2026-10-30 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/robert-caro)

**Thesis:** Caro says big things get built when a rare political genius refuses to accept that they can't be. Moses did it with public works and Johnson with civil rights. The risk is what that power does to the person who holds it. Moses began as an idealist and hardened into a man who evicted tens of thousands of people. Johnson's persuasion worked because of the leverage behind it. Asked for a middle ground between Moses and today's gridlock, Caro says, "I'm not sure there is a good spot."

- **The 1920s had the same "can't build" complaint.** Patel cites the Second Avenue subway: about 50 years of work, a mile and a half open, more than $4 billion spent. Caro says people said the same in Moses's day. Moses still built the road to Jones Beach, 27 miles from the city, through farms whose owners objected and before he had eminent domain.
- **The idealist hardens.** Young Moses pushed civil-service reform so promotion would come on merit. Later he built the Cross Bronx Expressway and evicted tens of thousands "without any relocation provisions." Bronx politician Henry Epstein proposed moving the route two blocks south. Moses destroyed him by making it known Epstein was having an affair.
- **Watching power drain away.** In Caro's early interviews, a secretary brought in a list of callers afterwards. By the last long one there were no calls. In 1962 Moses threatened to resign, as he had many times before, and Nelson Rockefeller said "I regretfully accept." Caro would ask a final question: "did you realize you were changing?"
- **Johnson's persuasion rested on leverage.** Senators knew he could give or withhold committee seats and bills. One senator, called at 5 a.m., said "oh, Lyndon, I was just lying here waiting for your call." Could Kennedy have passed Johnson's laws? Caro says no: he lacked the "desire to change America."
- **Craft and the archive.** Caro boils each book down to one sentence before he starts. *The Power Broker* manuscript ran 1,050,000 words, and 350,000 were cut. He did about 700 interviews for it alone. He gave his archive to the New York Historical Society for "a lot less money" than other offers, so it would stay in New York.
- **Loneliness.** Caro once drove 22 miles to an interview in the Hill Country without passing a single house. He turns his phone off every morning.

> "Behind his persuasion was the power." — Robert Caro

> "So whatever you thought of Moses, you felt the poignance of someone losing power." — Robert Caro

### Long Now — "Baratunde Thurston & Blaise Agüera y Arcas: On Interdependence"

**Guests:** Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Baratunde Thurston · **Aired:** 2026-11-04 · *No transcript — notes from show notes only (the show notes give a detailed recap, quoted lines as printed there).* · [Listen](https://longnow.org/talks/02026-thurston-aguera-y-arcas)

**Thesis:** What can a story of symbiosis and interdependence teach us about living alongside AI? Agüera y Arcas calls individual human intelligence a myth. In his view, we have always been a collective intelligence entwined with our technologies. Thurston agrees that union is in our nature. His worry is speed, and he asks who controls the loss of capabilities and agency.

- **Symbiogenesis.** Agüera y Arcas argues that life grew more complex by merging, not splitting. Separate organisms fuse into new forms, and each merger speeds change by opening new possibilities.
- **A speed problem.** Thurston frames the current acceleration as a speed problem. People need time and new stories to adjust, because "when you invent the ship, you invent the shipwreck." He says caring for one another through acceleration must be the prime directive.
- **Against "robots should be slaves".** Responding to Joanna Bryson, Thurston says the master is also in chains: owning another dehumanizes the owner. We are relational beings, so how we treat AI will shape how we treat each other.
- **Interdependence over zero-sum.** "We've overdone independence," Thurston says, and "interdependence makes us richer." Agüera y Arcas wants to drop a zero-sum idea of human intelligence in which every AI advance lowers humanity's value. Over the long arc, he says, trading individual skills for collective capabilities pays off.
