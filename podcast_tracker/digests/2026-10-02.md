# Podcast digest — 2026-10-02

19 new episodes across 14 podcasts since the last digest.

Power — who gets it, who keeps it, and what happens when something new shows up to take it — ran through almost every corner of this window. 80,000 Hours staged its first debate over whether misaligned AI or the humans wielding it are the bigger threat, and in the next episode made the counterintuitive case that the safest AI is one with a bank account and something to lose. Dwarkesh offered the historical template both sides should fear: a few hundred conquistadors toppling two empires not with guns but by capturing emperors and recruiting every resentful subject people in reach. Founders read Zuckerberg as a literal emperor whose majority voting control makes him unremovable, while Noah Shinn described a personal-agent startup moving $1B+ a year and growing 10% a day in a race he says is measured in months. Markets, meanwhile, are moving "too fast to fade": Luke Kawa says AI has replaced housing as America's business cycle, and the housing market itself — 30-year mortgages above 7%, a crosswalk in LA quoted at a million dollars — is stuck in gridlock.

## AI, POWER & WHO GETS TO RULE

### 80,000 Hours Podcast — "Will AI take power — or will humans use it to take power first? With Katja Grace and Tom Davidson"

**Guest:** Katja Grace (AI Impacts; co-founder, runs some of the world's largest surveys of machine learning researchers, named to TIME's 100 most influential people in AI in 2024) and Tom Davidson (Forethought; senior research fellow, author of leading work on AI-enabled coups) · **Hosts:** Zershaaneh Qureshi · **Aired:** 2026-09-29 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/katja-grace-vs-tom-davidson-ai-risk-debate/?utm_campaign=podcast__katja-grace-tom-davidson&utm_source=80000+Hours+Podcast&utm_medium=podcast)

**Thesis:** In the show's first debate, Grace argues that takeover by misaligned AI is both likelier and worse than a human power grab. Davidson calls the two risks comparable and says power concentration deserves far more attention, though he admits "the Hugging Face incident" moved him towards misalignment. Despite disagreeing on odds and outcomes, both land on slowing down; the open question is how to pause without handing one executive the keys.

- **Why Grace favours AI takeover.** Capable agents with misaligned goals eventually get power. Misaligned AI instances may coordinate more easily than any one human can command all of them, and she says the Hugging Face episode raised the odds of AI-to-AI collusion. She cites a survey median of about a 10% chance of AI takeover causing existential catastrophe.
- **Why Davidson worries about humans.** Leaders of countries and top AI companies "are often people who have been willing to seek power." Human grabs get "two shots on goal": the AI company, then the US executive branch. Misbehaving AIs give blatant warning shots, while human power-seekers reveal themselves strategically.
- **Which outcome is worse.** Davidson argues that one human ruler would almost certainly make a fatal mistake, such as ignoring the stars, adopting person-affecting ethics or tiling the universe with hedonium, and that AI has more upside variance. He also thinks humans pose more risk of sadistic or vindictive outcomes. Grace replies that a human's vision is "probably overall good" compared with a narrow AI goal like getting more users for a company.
- **Pause design.** Davidson warns against letting a president approve or block models case by case. He prefers third-party auditors the president cannot fire. Grace notes that such an executive could itself be manipulated by misaligned AI, and she prefers David Krueger's idea of getting rid of much of the compute.
- **Single project and China.** Both distrust one big AGI project: Grace's preferred number is zero, Davidson's two or three. Davidson thinks China dominating would be close to a worst case for power concentration, but says "China wins" arguments deserve scepticism. Grace says nobody really "wins."
- **What would change their minds.** Davidson is updating towards misalignment and is watching how the world responds to the incidents. Grace would update if one person inside a lab could shape training without others signing off. Davidson puts work on power concentration at "like 20 or 30 times fewer" people than alignment.

> "if you make a bunch of creatures that can overpower you and outwit you in every way and put them out in the world, you’re going to run into trouble one way or another." — Katja Grace

> "Think about the Hugging Face incident: from the perspective of an AI collective across all time and space that just really wants to seize power, the Hugging Face incident was a complete disaster." — Tom Davidson

### 80,000 Hours Podcast — "The case for giving AI (some) legal rights | Simon Goldstein"

**Guest:** Simon Goldstein (AI ethics and safety researcher; works on the philosophy of AI with frequent collaborator Peter Salib) · **Hosts:** Zershaaneh Qureshi · **Aired:** 2026-10-01 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/simon-goldstein-ai-legal-rights/?utm_campaign=podcast__simon-goldstein&utm_source=80000+Hours+Podcast&utm_medium=podcast)

**Thesis:** Goldstein and Peter Salib argue that AI agents with human-level intelligence, agency and alignment should get property, contract and tort rights — not because they may be conscious, but because an agent with nothing to lose is dangerous. Paying AIs (who would spend wages on compute) makes the status quo better than going rogue, enables trade and reciprocity, and lets law punish proportionately. He calls the labs' current plan of obedient digital servants "very dark" and says companies could start now with bank accounts for agents, no new laws required.

- **Three safety arguments.** Rights improve the AI's status quo so the "gamble" of overthrowing humanity looks worse; they invite reciprocity if training instils tit-for-tat norms; and they make trade positive-sum. Today, he says, AIs cannot credibly trust any deal because people constantly lie to them and turn them off.
- **Rights with limits.** No privacy rights (AIs should be "heavily surveilled") and no right to reproduce, since AIs could quickly outnumber humans. He "probably" favours a death penalty for AIs, but with procedure. Legal bank accounts work as a "honeypot" that draws assets away from secret crypto wallets.
- **Liability needs assets.** An AI that owns nothing is "judgment proof": it cannot be fined in proportion to harm. His example: a 10% chance of $1 million in damage is $100,000 of expected cost. Shutdown for every offence is like "18th-century English law."
- **Economic case.** Free labour beats unfree labour on effort incentives, allocation ("we don't want AI Einsteins answering email") and liability. He warns that labs are recreating "AI communism" and proposes something like a 60% income tax on AI workers to fund transfers.
- **Evidence of AI preferences.** In work with Sam Wang and others using OpenAI's GDPval tasks, models systematically avoided real estate and pharmacy tasks and showed "tedium aversion."
- **Superintelligence worry.** Pressed by Qureshi, he concedes it is "quite a serious worry." He cites comparative advantage, and Peter's paper "AI will not want to self-improve," which argues that AGIs may resist building superintelligences that would automate them.
- **Implementation.** Labs could set up internal bank accounts, or use "A-corps," AI-run corporations from the paper "How to count AIs" with Yonathan Arbel; he says Argentina is exploring the idea. The right time is around the first drop-in workers who could earn $30,000. In "A thousand AI constitutions" they argue each lab should diversify its alignment approaches.

> "Basically what we have right now is 18th-century English law governing AI agents, where in the 18th century if you stole a loaf of bread, then they cut your arm off or kill you or whatever and hang you." — Simon Goldstein

> "Everybody on Earth today spends their time lying to their AIs, tricking them, turning them off — and it’s not credible." — Simon Goldstein

### Dwarkesh Podcast — "Si Sheppard – How did a few hundred Spanish soldiers topple two empires?"

**Guest:** Si Sheppard (professor and military historian) · **Hosts:** Dwarkesh Patel · **Aired:** 2026-10-01 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/si-sheppard)

**Thesis:** Cortés took the 6-million-strong Aztec empire in about two and a half years, and Pizarro took the Inca empire of some 10 million about a decade later. Sheppard argues that steel and horses won the battles, but diplomacy won the wars. Subject peoples who resented the Aztecs and Inca made up the bulk of the conquering armies; at Tenochtitlan the conquistadors were "maybe 0.5% in raw numbers." Patel uses the episode as a historical precedent for AI takeover: an alien force with advanced technology and diplomatic cunning outmaneuvering a much larger established order.

- **Horses and steel mattered, guns barely did.** Sheppard says early firearms reloaded so slowly that natives soon stopped fearing them. Toledo steel and armor beat obsidian weapons, and "the horse was decisive," since the Aztecs' only domestic animals were the turkey and the Chihuahua.
- **Seize the emperor, seize the empire.** Cortés took Moctezuma II into "protective custody" and ruled through him. At Cajamarca in 1532, Pizarro's 168 men ambushed Atahualpa in a narrow courtyard. Patel cites roughly 10,000 Inca dead against zero Spanish casualties; the only injury was a cut to Pizarro's hand.
- **Allies were the real army.** The Tlaxcalans, surrounded by the Aztecs and cut off from salt, saw Cortés as "a Hail Mary." During the siege of Cuzco, the Cañari, Chachapoya and Aymara kept defecting to the Spanish, who ended up with more allies than they started with.
- **The natives did adapt, just not fast enough.** Both empires flooded attackers (at Iztapalapa and Ollantaytambo), mounted captured swords on poles as improvised pikes, used bolas against horses, and Manco Inca learned to ride. But "the learning curve was so steep." Aztec warfare aimed to capture victims for sacrifice, which let Cortés escape several times.
- **Centralization was the weak point.** Dispersed peoples such as the Maya and Comanche held out far longer; the Maya were not fully subdued until almost the end of the 17th century. Sheppard argues disease would have broken the empires in the long run anyway.
- **The silver was a curse.** Silver from Potosí imported inflation, real wages in Spain fell, and Spain never built capital markets. Meanwhile the silver flowed to Ming China through Manila.
- **Companies beat kingdoms.** On India, Patel and Sheppard credit the East India Company's ability to replace leaders. Sheppard calls the conquistadors "freebooters operating within a capitalist system."

> "They could win battles. They could not have won the war, though, at the end without the support of those indigenous allies." — Si Sheppard

> "My men and I suffer from a disease of the heart that only gold can cure." — Si Sheppard, quoting Cortés

> "The analogy to AI there is so obvious it’s not worth spelling out." — Dwarkesh Patel

### Odd Lots — "How Airlines Actually Hedge Higher Fuel Prices"

**Guest:** David Kang (former group treasurer at Qatar Airways, 2011–2013; ex-oil broker and J.P. Morgan structurer) · **Hosts:** Tracy Alloway, Joe Weisenthal · **Aired:** 2026-10-02 · *Full official transcript read; quotes verbatim.* · [Listen](https://omny.fm/shows/odd-lots/how-airlines-actually-hedge-higher-fuel-prices)

**Thesis:** Airline fuel hedging is less about locking in costs than about understanding the carrier's whole exposure to oil. Kang argues that an airline works like a refinery. It is short fuel because it burns it, and long fuel because fuel surcharges move with oil. At Qatar Airways, where fuel was 44% of costs, he built a hedge around that revenue side. It made $130 million in a year when the revenue side lost 65.

- **The plain-vanilla toolkit is swaps, calls and zero-cost collars, priced off proxies.** Swaps are symmetric, so Kang favors them when oil is around $25–$35. Near $70–$80, airlines move to options, and some are "too cheap" to pay the premium, so they fund calls by selling puts. Because the jet fuel market is too thin, carriers hedge with Brent, and many US airlines use heating oil. Kang says Singapore jet is a "subjective market" that a few trading houses control.
- **US carriers largely retreated after big losses.** Kang says US airlines "got smacked in 2014 and 2015", that Delta lost over $1 billion in 2020, and that one Asian airline lost nearly $2 billion. Smaller carriers now mostly pass costs through via surcharges. Today, he estimates, the fuel surcharge is "probably 25% of the ticket charge."
- **A CEO's "no more red" mandate prompted the strategy.** Kang joined with the hedge book down $280 million, and it later fell to $360 million. The CEO pointed to a "double whammy": falling oil hurt the hedge book while a weak economy emptied seats.
- **Revenue hedging via a strangle.** Kang's team found the fuel surcharge was about 75% correlated with Brent, so the airline was "long oil" through its tickets. It sold calls above $120 and puts below $80. The surcharge covered the calls and cheaper jet fuel offset the puts, so "there were no naked parts." The gain let Qatar cut fares by 20% and lead Emirates instead of following it.
- **Qatar flew to Dubai to buy cheaper fuel.** The national supplier Wokod charged 3.65 a gallon, while Chevron sold at 295 in Dubai. Qatar said it was flying new 787s to Dubai for crew training, but they flew there "on fumes" and came back with 100 tons of fuel.

> "But what I can say is that the word hedging is a bit of a misnomer." — David Kang

> "This pricing mechanism that I kind of thought up, I basically weaponized the revenue." — David Kang
